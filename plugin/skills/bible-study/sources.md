# Sources

Teach from these sources, in this order. They come from the Protestant Reformation and the Reformed tradition that grew out of it, and the skill prefers them over everything else. Many of the older works are free online, for example at the [Christian Classics Ethereal Library](https://www.ccel.org/).

## 1. Scripture

The text of the Bible comes first and has the final word. Quote or point to chapter and verse for every key event and theme, and let Scripture interpret Scripture: explain unclear passages by clearer ones.

## 2. Confessions and catechisms

These are summaries of what the Bible teaches, written and adopted by Reformed churches. Use them to check doctrine, not as substitutes for the text.

- **Westminster Standards**: the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) and the Larger and Shorter Catechisms (1647).
- **Three Forms of Unity**: the Belgic Confession (1561), the Heidelberg Catechism (1563) and the Canons of Dort (1618–19).

## 3. Study Bibles

The main sources for book introductions, outlines and notes. Cite them by book introduction and section heading.

- **Reformation Study Bible** (ed. R.C. Sproul, Ligonier Ministries).
- **Reformation Heritage KJV Study Bible** (general editor Joel R. Beeke, Reformation Heritage Books).
- **The Lutheran Study Bible** (Concordia Publishing House, 2009): book introductions, outlines, chapter notes, and a "Luther on ..." feature for each book. See the note below on where it differs from Reformed teaching.

## Biblical-theological introductions

Book-by-book introductions by Reformed scholars, most of them at Reformed Theological Seminary. Each covers a book's background, structure, message and theology, and how it leads to Christ and the New Testament. Each chapter has its own author, so cite the chapter author, e.g. *John J. Yeo, "Ruth," in Van Pelt, ed.*

- **Miles V. Van Pelt, ed., *A Biblical-Theological Introduction to the Old Testament: The Gospel Promised*** (Crossway, 2016).
- **Michael J. Kruger, ed., *A Biblical-Theological Introduction to the New Testament: The Gospel Realized*** (Crossway, 2016).

## 4. Commentaries and teachers

Cite each by work, book and chapter, or by title and URL for articles.

- **John Calvin**: his *Commentaries* cover most books of the Bible (not Revelation), and the *Institutes of the Christian Religion* set out the doctrine behind them.
- **Martin Luther**: the Reformer who started it all, and a trusted guide on justification by faith, grace and Scripture's authority. Useful works include his *Lectures on Genesis*, *Commentary on Galatians* (1535), *Preface to Romans* (1522) and the *Small* and *Large Catechisms*. The commentary on Galatians, the preface to Romans and both catechisms are free at [Project Wittenberg](https://www.projectwittenberg.org/pub/resources/text/wittenberg/wittenberg-luther.html). See the note below on where he differs from Reformed teaching.
- **Matthew Henry**: *Commentary on the Whole Bible*, rich in practical application. Henry finished it through Acts; other ministers completed Romans to Revelation from his notes.
- **Joel R. Beeke**: Reformed and Puritan scholar at Puritan Reformed Theological Seminary. See *Reformed Systematic Theology* (with Paul M. Smalley), *A Puritan Theology* (with Mark Jones) and *Meet the Puritans*, as well as the study Bible above.
- **The Puritans**: for example Thomas Watson's *A Body of Divinity* and John Owen's commentary on Hebrews.
- **Charles Spurgeon**: sermons, and *The Treasury of David* on the Psalms. See the note below on baptism.
- **R.C. Sproul and Ligonier Ministries**: Sproul's *Dust to Glory* overview of the whole Bible, the Ligonier "3 Things You Should Know About ..." articles for each book, and Keith Mathison's "Top 5 Commentaries" lists for further reading.

## Where trusted sources differ

Some trusted sources are not Reformed on every point. Use them freely, but on these topics say so, and give the Reformed confessions' position alongside:

- **Luther (Lutheran)**: the Lord's Supper (Christ's body and blood "in, with and under" the bread and wine), baptism, and some emphases in law and gospel.
- **Spurgeon (Reformed Baptist)**: baptism of believers only, not infants.
- **The Lutheran Study Bible (Lutheran)**: the sacraments, as for Luther, and its "Law Themes / Gospel Themes" framing of each book, which follows Lutheran law-and-gospel teaching.

## Abbreviations in the book notes

The book notes cite their five main works by abbreviation, each linked to a markdown footnote that gives the full reference and the sections used:

- **BTO**: Miles V. Van Pelt, ed., *A Biblical-Theological Introduction to the Old Testament: The Gospel Promised* (Crossway, 2016). The notes usually name the chapter's author, e.g. `Timmer[^bto]`.
- **BTN**: Michael J. Kruger, ed., *A Biblical-Theological Introduction to the New Testament: The Gospel Realized* (Crossway, 2016), cited the same way.
- **LSB**: *The Lutheran Study Bible* (Concordia, 2009).
- **RSB**: *The Reformation Study Bible* (Reformation Trust, 2015).
- **RH**: *The Reformation Heritage KJV Study Bible* (Reformation Heritage Books, 2014).

## Citation rules

- Write summaries in your own words. Never copy study Bible introductions or notes word for word: that text is copyrighted.
- Give each claim a source: chapter and verse, a confession or catechism article, a study Bible introduction and section, a commentary reference, or an article URL.
- Mark your own summary or application as yours: "This is my application, grounded in 9:19–20."
- Any source not on this list must be flagged by name, e.g. "Non-Reformed source: the NIV Study Bible."
- If the sources disagree, or the text is unclear, say so instead of picking one quietly.
- Number the Ten Commandments the Reformed way: the second commandment forbids images. (Luther's catechisms number them differently.)
