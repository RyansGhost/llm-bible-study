# Mark

## Summary

Mark is the shortest and fastest-moving Gospel. It opens with "the beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1) and races from one scene to the next ("immediately"). Jesus shows his authority over demons, disease, nature and sin, yet keeps telling people to be quiet about him. Halfway through, Peter confesses him as the Christ, and Jesus begins teaching that the Son of Man must suffer, die and rise. The Gospel climaxes at the cross, where a Roman centurion confesses, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39).

## Setting

- **When:** Jesus' public ministry, from his baptism to his resurrection (about AD 27–30/33). Gladd notes several scholars date the writing to the late 50s or early 60s, before Matthew and Luke and before Jerusalem's fall in AD 70. The Reformation Study Bible agrees it was written before AD 70, and earlier still if Luke and Acts were finished by about AD 62. The early church said it was written for the church in Rome: it explains Jewish customs and Aramaic words for Gentile readers (7:3–4; 5:41), uses Latin terms, and names Rufus, probably known in Rome (15:21; Rom 16:13).
- **Where:** the Jordan, Galilee (especially Capernaum and the lake), Gentile regions (Decapolis, Tyre and Sidon, Caesarea Philippi), the journey south, and Jerusalem.
- **Who wrote it:** John Mark, not one of the Twelve. The Jerusalem church met in his mother's house (Acts 12:12); he deserted Paul and Barnabas on their first journey but was later valued by Paul (Acts 13:13; 15:37–39; 2 Tim 4:11), and was with Peter in Rome (1 Pet 5:13). Early tradition from Papias says Mark wrote down Peter's preaching as "Peter's interpreter", which Gladd uses to explain how a non-apostle could write eyewitness-based testimony. Forgers chose famous names, not a figure like Mark (RSB). The young man who fled naked at Jesus' arrest (14:51–52) may be Mark himself.
- **Where it sits:** the second Gospel. Most scholars think it was the first written, and that Matthew and Luke used it. Gladd explains the Gospels as ancient biographies grounded in eyewitness testimony, written for the wider church; the RSB adds that they have been called passion narratives with long introductions, and that Mark may be the first to call a written account a "gospel" (1:1). The oldest manuscripts end at 16:8; the longer ending (16:9–20) seems to come from another hand.

## Structure

Gladd notes the Synoptic Gospels move geographically: from the Jordan to Galilee, where Jesus is welcomed, to Jerusalem, where he is rejected and killed. Peter's confession (8:29) is the watershed. The Reformation Study Bible outlines it as preparation (1:1–13), the disclosure of the Christ (1:14–8:26), his suffering (8:27–15:47), and the resurrection (16). Mark often "sandwiches" one story inside another so each explains the other, such as the fig tree around the temple cleansing (11:12–21) and Peter's denial around Jesus' trial (14:53–72).

- 1:1–13: Prologue: John the Baptist, Jesus' baptism and temptation
- 1:14–8:21: Jesus' authority in Galilee and beyond: teaching, healing, exorcisms, opposition, and the disciples' slowness
- 8:22–10:52: On the way: Peter's confession and three predictions of the passion, framed by two healings of blind men
- 11–13: In Jerusalem: the entry, the temple, debates, and the end-times discourse
- 14–16: The passion and the empty tomb

## Chapter by chapter

- **Mark 1:** "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God". John prepares the way, and Jesus is baptised and tempted. He calls fishermen, casts out an unclean spirit in Capernaum, heals many, and cleanses a leper.
- **Mark 2:** A paralysed man is lowered through a roof, and Jesus forgives and heals him. He calls Levi, eats with sinners, answers questions about fasting, and declares himself Lord of the Sabbath.
- **Mark 3:** Jesus heals a withered hand on the Sabbath, and the Pharisees plot. He appoints the Twelve. The scribes say he is possessed. His true family are those who do God's will.
- **Mark 4:** The parables of the sower, the lamp, the growing seed and the mustard seed. Jesus calms a storm: "Who then is this?"
- **Mark 5:** Jesus frees a man possessed by "Legion", heals a woman who touches his garment, and raises Jairus' daughter.
- **Mark 6:** He is rejected at Nazareth and sends out the Twelve. Herod beheads John. Jesus feeds five thousand and walks on the water.
- **Mark 7:** Human tradition against God's command, and what truly defiles. A Syrophoenician woman's faith. A deaf man healed: "Ephphatha".
- **Mark 8:** He feeds four thousand and heals a blind man in two stages. Peter confesses, "You are the Christ". Jesus first predicts his suffering and calls disciples to take up their cross.
- **Mark 9:** The transfiguration. A father cries, "I believe; help my unbelief!", and his son is healed. The second prediction, and who is greatest.
- **Mark 10:** Divorce, children and the rich man. The third prediction. "The Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (10:45). Blind Bartimaeus healed.
- **Mark 11:** The triumphal entry, the cursed fig tree, the cleansing of the temple, and the question of Jesus' authority.
- **Mark 12:** The wicked tenants, taxes to Caesar, the resurrection, the greatest commandment, and the poor widow's offering.
- **Mark 13:** Jesus foretells the temple's destruction and his return, and calls disciples to stay awake.
- **Mark 14:** A woman anoints Jesus at Bethany. Judas agrees to betray him. The Last Supper, Gethsemane, the arrest, the trial before the council, and Peter's three denials.
- **Mark 15:** Jesus before Pilate, Barabbas released, Jesus mocked and crucified. "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" The temple curtain tears, and the centurion confesses him as the Son of God. Joseph of Arimathea buries him.
- **Mark 16:** The women find the tomb empty, and a young man tells them, "He has risen". They flee in fear (16:8). Verses 9–20, a longer ending, are missing from the earliest manuscripts.

## Key people

- **Jesus:** the Christ, Son of God, and suffering Son of Man.
- **John the Baptist:** the forerunner.
- **Peter:** the leading disciple, who confesses Jesus and denies him. Mark's Gospel likely preserves his preaching.
- **James and John:** the sons of Zebedee, part of Jesus' inner circle.
- **The Twelve:** often slow to understand.
- **The scribes, Pharisees, chief priests and Herodians:** Jesus' opponents.
- **Jairus and the bleeding woman:** examples of faith.
- **Bartimaeus:** a blind beggar who follows Jesus.
- **Pilate:** the Roman governor.
- **The centurion:** a Gentile who confesses Jesus at the cross.
- **Mary Magdalene and the women:** witnesses of the death, burial and empty tomb.

## Themes

- **Who is Jesus?** The question runs through the book ("Who then is this?", 4:41), answered by the demons, by Peter (8:29), and by the centurion (15:39).
- **The messianic secret:** Jesus often tells people to keep quiet about him (1:34, 44; 8:30). Gladd discusses why: Jesus redefined messiahship around suffering, not political triumph, and his identity becomes fully clear only at the cross and resurrection.
- **The suffering Messiah:** Gladd sums up Mark's purpose as reworking messianic hopes: the Messiah's reign comes through suffering and apparent defeat (8:31; 10:45).
- **Isaiah's new exodus:** Gladd notes the influence of Isaiah's promise of a second exodus on Mark, from its opening quotation (1:2–3) onward.
- **Discipleship:** following Jesus means taking up the cross (8:34). The disciples' repeated failures show the need for grace.
- **Authority:** Jesus acts with God's own authority over sin, Sabbath, nature, demons and death. The gospel itself is God's power over evil and sickness (1:27; compare Rom 1:16).
- **Glory through the cross:** against Rome's idea of power, the Son of Man's glory is his sacrifice, and greatness among his people means serving (10:42–45) (RSB).
- **The Gentiles:** the temple is a house of prayer for all nations (11:17), and the first person to confess Jesus as Son of God after his death is a Roman soldier (15:39).
- **Urgency:** the frequent "immediately" gives the Gospel its pace.

## How it points to Christ

Mark is the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God (1:1). It shows Jesus as both the mighty Lord who commands wind and waves and the Servant who came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (10:45), fulfilling Isaiah 53. His death opens the way to God: the temple curtain tears in two (15:38). The first full human confession of who he is comes from a Gentile soldier watching him die (15:39). The empty tomb and the message "He has risen" call every reader to faith.

## Common mix-ups

- Mark is John Mark, Barnabas' cousin, who left Paul and Barnabas on their first journey (Acts 13:13; 15:37–39; Col 4:10) and was later useful to Paul (2 Tim 4:11) and close to Peter (1 Pet 5:13).
- Mark has no birth story; it starts with John the Baptist.
- Mark is the shortest Gospel, but often gives the most vivid details.
- Mark 16:9–20 is not in the earliest and best manuscripts. Most modern Bibles mark it, and Gladd discusses the problem.
- The young man who flees naked at Jesus' arrest (14:51–52) is sometimes thought to be Mark himself, but the text doesn't say.
- The "Ephphatha" healing (7:31–37) and the two-stage healing of a blind man (8:22–26) appear only in Mark.

## Going deeper

- **The messianic secret.** Why does Jesus silence demons and people he heals? Gladd surveys the explanations. Read: Gladd, The "Messianic Secret".
- **Mark's ending.** Does Mark end at 16:8, with the women afraid and silent? What would such an ending say to readers? Gladd discusses the manuscript evidence.
- **Isaiah's new exodus in Mark.** Gladd draws on the argument that Isaiah's new exodus shapes Mark's Gospel, starting with 1:2–3. Trace the journey from wilderness to Jerusalem.
- **Discipleship and failure.** The Twelve misunderstand, argue over greatness, sleep in Gethsemane and flee. How does that encourage struggling disciples today?
- **"Ransom for many" (10:45).** Connect this key verse to Isaiah 53 and to the cup and the cross in Mark 14–15.

## Q&A

1. **Q:** How does Mark's Gospel begin?

   **A:** "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1).

2. **Q:** Who was Mark?

   **A:** John Mark, a companion of Paul, Barnabas and Peter, not one of the Twelve (Acts 12:12, 25; 1 Pet 5:13).

3. **Q:** Whose preaching does early tradition say Mark recorded?

   **A:** Peter's.

4. **Q:** What word does Mark use often to keep the story moving?

   **A:** "Immediately", again and again (e.g. 1:10, 12, 18).

5. **Q:** What did the father of the demon-possessed boy cry out?

   **A:** "I believe; help my unbelief!" (9:24).

6. **Q:** Why did the Son of Man come, according to 10:45?

   **A:** Not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.

7. **Q:** Which blind man followed Jesus on the road?

   **A:** Bartimaeus (10:46–52).

8. **Q:** What did the centurion say at the cross?

   **A:** "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39).

9. **Q:** What happened to the temple curtain when Jesus died?

   **A:** It was torn in two from top to bottom (15:38).

10. **Q:** Why do many Bibles mark Mark 16:9–20?

    **A:** It isn't found in the earliest manuscripts.
