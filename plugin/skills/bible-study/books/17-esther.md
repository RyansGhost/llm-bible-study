## Summary

Esther tells how God saved his scattered people from genocide in the Persian empire without his name being mentioned once. A Jewish orphan becomes queen, her cousin Mordecai refuses to bow to the proud official Haman, and Haman plots to destroy every Jew. At the risk of her life, Esther goes to the king, and in a series of dramatic reversals Haman is hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai. The Jews are delivered, and the feast of Purim celebrates it.

## Setting

- **When:** the reign of Ahasuerus, better known as Xerxes I (486–465 BC), roughly 483–473 BC. This falls between the temple's completion (Ezra 6) and Ezra's return (Ezra 7).
- **Where:** Susa, the Persian winter capital, and the empire from India to Cush. These are Jews who stayed in exile rather than returning to Jerusalem.
- **Who wrote it:** unknown. Lee and the Lutheran Study Bible both place it around 400 BC, before Alexander the Great. The author mentions royal chronicles (10:2).
- **Where it sits:** in English Bibles, the last of the history books. In the Hebrew Bible it is one of the five festival scrolls (Megilloth), read at Purim, about a month before Passover (Lee).

## Structure

Lee suggests three ways of seeing the book's shape:

- **Banquets:** the turning points happen at feasts: the king's feasts (1), Esther's two banquets (5; 7), and the feasting of Purim (9).
- **Plot:** the setting (1–2), the threat and its reversal (3–9:19), and the establishment of Purim (9:20–10:3).
- **Reversals:** what Haman planned for the Jews falls on his own head (9:1).

In outline:

- 1–2: Vashti deposed; Esther becomes queen; Mordecai uncovers a plot
- 3–4: Haman's decree to destroy the Jews; Mordecai's appeal to Esther
- 5–7: Esther's banquets; Mordecai honoured; Haman exposed and hanged
- 8–10: A counter-decree; the Jews' victory; Purim; Mordecai's greatness

## Chapter by chapter

- **Esther 1:** King Ahasuerus throws lavish feasts in Susa. Queen Vashti refuses to come and display her beauty, and she is removed as queen.
- **Esther 2:** The king searches for a new queen. Esther, a Jewish orphan raised by her cousin Mordecai, is chosen but keeps her Jewish identity secret. Mordecai uncovers a plot to kill the king, and it is written in the royal records.
- **Esther 3:** Haman the Agagite is promoted, and Mordecai refuses to bow to him. Enraged, Haman casts lots (pur) for a date and gets a royal decree to destroy all Jews on the 13th of Adar.
- **Esther 4:** Mordecai mourns in sackcloth. He urges Esther to plead with the king: "who knows whether you have not come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" (4:14). She asks the Jews to fast, saying, "if I perish, I perish" (4:16).
- **Esther 5:** Esther approaches the king unsummoned and he accepts her. She invites him and Haman to a banquet, then another. Haman, furious at Mordecai, builds a huge gallows for him.
- **Esther 6:** The king can't sleep, has the chronicles read, and learns Mordecai was never rewarded. Haman, thinking the honour is for himself, ends up leading Mordecai through the city in royal robes.
- **Esther 7:** At the second banquet Esther reveals that she is Jewish and that Haman plans to destroy her people. Haman is hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai.
- **Esther 8:** Mordecai receives Haman's position. Since the first decree can't be revoked, a new decree lets the Jews defend themselves.
- **Esther 9:** On the appointed day the Jews defeat their enemies and Haman's ten sons die. Mordecai and Esther establish the yearly feast of Purim.
- **Esther 10:** Mordecai, second only to the king, seeks the welfare of his people.

## Key people

- **Esther (Hadassah):** a Jewish orphan who becomes queen and risks her life for her people.
- **Mordecai:** Esther's cousin and guardian, a Benjaminite who refuses to bow to Haman.
- **Ahasuerus (Xerxes I):** king of Persia: powerful, impulsive and easily swayed.
- **Vashti:** the queen deposed for refusing the king.
- **Haman the Agagite:** the king's proud chief official and enemy of the Jews.
- **Zeresh:** Haman's wife, who suggests the gallows.
- **Hegai:** the official in charge of the women, who favours Esther.

- Vashti refuses the king and is deposed (1)

## Themes

- **God's hidden providence:** God is never named, yet he is everywhere behind the "coincidences": Esther's rise, the timing of Mordecai's discovery, the king's sleepless night, and Haman's arrival at just the wrong moment (6:1–4).
- **Reversal:** the book is full of turnarounds. The Jews' mourning becomes feasting, Haman honours the man he planned to hang, and he dies on his own gallows (9:1, 22). Lee makes reversal one of the book's key structures.
- **Courage and responsibility:** God's sovereignty doesn't remove Esther's responsibility to act (4:13–16).
- **The ancient enemy:** Mordecai is a Benjaminite from Saul's family (2:5), and Haman an Agagite, from the line of the Amalekite king Saul failed to destroy (1 Sam 15). The old conflict resurfaces.
- **God's people in exile:** Jews living far from the land are still God's people, and he preserves them.
- **Remembering deliverance:** Purim keeps God's rescue alive in Israel's memory, as Passover does.

## How it points to Christ

Esther's story is about God preserving the people from whom the Messiah would come. Had Haman succeeded, the line of promise would have been wiped out. Esther goes to the king uninvited, at the risk of her life, to plead for her condemned people. That foreshadows Christ, who went further: he gave his life to save his people, and he intercedes for us before the King (Heb 7:25).

The great reversal, the enemy defeated by the very weapon he prepared, finds its deepest fulfilment at the cross. The evil meant to destroy Jesus became the means of salvation, and he disarmed the powers and triumphed over them (Col 2:15).

## Common mix-ups

- Mordecai is Esther's cousin, not her uncle (2:7, 15).
- God's name never appears in the Hebrew text of Esther. The longer Greek version adds prayers that mention him; Protestants regard those additions as non-canonical.
- Ahasuerus and Xerxes are the same king. Esther happens between Ezra 6 and Ezra 7.
- Haman is an Agagite, not an Amalekite king. He is a descendant connected with Agag (1 Sam 15).
- Purim (Esther 9) is a different feast from Passover. It is named after the lots (pur) Haman cast.
- Esther was not in Jerusalem; the whole story takes place in Persia.
- Vashti is the deposed queen in chapter 1; Esther replaces her.

## Going deeper

- **Why isn't God mentioned?** Lee discusses Esther's canonical struggles and Luther's well-known dislike of the book. Its silence about God may itself be the point: God works invisibly through ordinary events. Read: Lee, Canonical Matters.
- **Is Esther historical?** Many scholars call it historical fiction: there is no Persian record of a Queen Esther, and Xerxes' known queen was Amestris. Lee reviews the objections. How might they be answered, and what is at stake?
- **"For such a time as this" (4:14).** Mordecai is sure deliverance will come from somewhere, yet he urges Esther to act. How do God's sovereignty and human responsibility fit together here?
- **The Jews' self-defence (9).** Is the violence of chapter 9 vengeance or legitimate defence? Note that they took no plunder (9:10, 15–16), unlike Saul with Agag.
- **Reversals everywhere.** Trace the pairs: Vashti and Esther, Haman and Mordecai, the two decrees, mourning and feasting. What does this structure teach about how God governs history?

## Q&A

1. Who was the king in Esther, and what is his better-known name? Ahasuerus, also known as Xerxes I.
2. Why was Queen Vashti removed? She refused to come when the king summoned her to display her beauty (1:12).
3. How was Esther related to Mordecai? She was his cousin, whom he raised as his daughter (2:7).
4. Why did Haman hate Mordecai? Mordecai refused to bow down to him (3:2–6).
5. What does "Purim" mean? "Lots", from the lot (pur) Haman cast to choose the day of destruction (3:7; 9:24–26).
6. What did Mordecai say to persuade Esther? "Who knows whether you have not come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" (4:14).
7. What did Esther say before going to the king? "If I perish, I perish" (4:16).
8. What happened during the king's sleepless night? He learned Mordecai had saved his life and had never been rewarded (6:1–3).
9. How did Haman die? Hanged on the gallows he had built for Mordecai (7:10).
10. What is unusual about God in Esther? He is never mentioned by name, yet his providence runs through the whole story.
