# Philippians

## Summary

Philippians is a warm letter from Paul in prison to a church that had partnered with him in the gospel from the start. Lowe reads it as a letter of friendship and partnership, and also of possible bereavement: Paul doesn't know whether he will live or die, so he writes to prepare them. He rejoices that his chains have advanced the gospel ("to live is Christ, and to die is gain", 1:21). He urges humility and unity by pointing to Christ, who emptied himself to the point of death on a cross and was exalted. He counts everything loss compared with knowing Christ, and he thanks them for their gift, having learned contentment in all circumstances.

## Setting

- **When:** about AD 60–62, during Paul's imprisonment, most likely in Rome near its end, about AD 61: the "imperial guard" (1:13), "Caesar's household" (4:22) and the real prospect of execution (1:20–26) fit a trial at the highest level. Some scholars suggest Caesarea or Ephesus, though Acts records no imprisonment in Ephesus (Reformation Study Bible).
- **Where:** Philippi, a Roman colony in Macedonia on the main road between Rome and the east, named after Philip of Macedon and settled partly by retired soldiers who enjoyed Roman citizenship. That gives extra force to Paul's language about citizenship (1:27; 3:20). Paul founded the first church in Europe there (Acts 16: Lydia, the slave girl, and the jailer) and visited again later (Acts 20:1–6). The church was mostly Gentile.
- **Who wrote it:** Paul, with Timothy (1:1).
- **Where it sits:** one of the Prison Epistles. Lowe cautions against overstating conflict in the church. Paul's imprisonment, Epaphroditus' near-fatal illness, and the letter's farewell tone are the clearer background. Lowe compares it with farewell speeches in Scripture (John 13–17; Acts 20). The RSB also notes real pressures: persecution (1:27–30), false teachers (3:2–21) and quarrels (4:2–3). The sharp turn at 3:2 doesn't mean chapter 3 came from another letter; its themes and wording tie it to the rest.

## Structure

Many take 1:27 as the letter's main point: live worthy of the gospel, standing firm in one spirit, striving together for the faith. The Reformation Study Bible's outline:

- 1:1–2: Greeting
- 1:3–11: Thanksgiving and prayer for their partnership
- 1:12–2:30: The truth of the gospel: Paul's circumstances (to live is Christ, to die is gain); live worthy of the gospel in unity and humility, after Christ's example (2:5–11); Timothy and Epaphroditus
- 3:1–4:1: Truth against error: no confidence in the flesh; knowing Christ; pressing on; heavenly citizenship
- 4:2–9: Appeals: agreement, joy, prayer instead of anxiety, right thinking
- 4:10–20: Thanks for their gift, and contentment
- 4:21–23: Final greeting and blessing

## Chapter by chapter

- **Philippians 1:** Paul thanks God for their partnership in the gospel and is sure God will complete the good work he began (1:6). His imprisonment has advanced the gospel. "For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain" (1:21). He calls them to live worthy of the gospel, striving together.
- **Philippians 2:** Be of one mind, humble, putting others first. Have the mind of Christ, who emptied himself, took the form of a servant, and became obedient to death on a cross. God has highly exalted him, so every knee will bow and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord (2:5–11). "Work out your own salvation … for it is God who works in you" (2:12–13). Shine as lights. Timothy and Epaphroditus are commended.
- **Philippians 3:** Rejoice in the Lord. Beware of those who boast in circumcision. Paul had every reason for confidence in the flesh, but counts it all loss for the surpassing worth of knowing Christ. He doesn't have a righteousness of his own from the law, but the righteousness from God through faith (3:9). He presses on toward the goal. Our citizenship is in heaven.
- **Philippians 4:** Euodia and Syntyche must agree in the Lord. "Rejoice in the Lord always". Don't be anxious, but pray with thanksgiving, and God's peace will guard your hearts (4:6–7). Think on whatever is true, honourable and lovely. Paul has learned contentment in plenty and in want: "I can do all things through him who strengthens me" (4:13). He thanks them for their gifts.

## Key people

- **Paul:** imprisoned, joyful, and facing possible death.
- **Timothy:** Paul's trusted co-worker, sent to them soon (2:19–24).
- **Epaphroditus:** the Philippians' messenger, who nearly died bringing their gift (2:25–30; 4:18).
- **Euodia and Syntyche:** two women co-workers urged to agree (4:2–3).
- **"Caesar's household":** believers in the imperial service who send greetings (4:22).
- **The Philippian church:** Paul's faithful partners in the gospel from the first day (1:5).

## Key events

- The founding of the church at Philippi (Acts 16)
- The Philippians' gifts to Paul (4:15–18)
- Epaphroditus' illness and recovery (2:25–30)
- Paul's imprisonment and uncertain future (1:12–26)

## Themes

- **Joy in all circumstances:** "joy" and "rejoice" come sixteen times, in a letter written from prison (1:18; 2:17–18; 3:1; 4:4), grounded in the peace of God (4:4–7).
- **Partnership in the gospel:** the church shares in Paul's ministry by prayer, giving and suffering (1:5, 7; 4:14–16). Lowe reads the letter as one of friendship and partnership.
- **Facing death:** Paul is ready for either outcome, because Christ is his life and death is gain (1:20–26). Lowe notes the farewell tone.
- **The mind of Christ:** humility and self-giving love modelled on Christ's own (2:1–11), and seen in Timothy and Epaphroditus (2:19–30). Christ "emptied himself" not by giving up any part of his deity, but by adding a true human nature and taking a servant's form (2:7; compare Westminster Larger Catechism 36). Calling him "Lord" identifies him with the God of Israel (2:10–11; Isa 45:23).
- **Righteousness through faith:** not from the law, but from God through faith in Christ (3:9).
- **Pressing on:** the Christian life as a race toward the prize (3:12–14). We work out our salvation because God is at work in us (2:12–13), and he will finish what he began (1:6).
- **Heavenly citizenship:** in a Roman colony proud of its citizenship, believers' true citizenship is in heaven (3:20).
- **Contentment and peace:** prayer replaces anxiety, and contentment is learned in Christ (4:6–7, 11–13). Their gift to Paul is a fragrant offering, like the sacrifices of the temple (4:18).

## How it points to Christ

The great hymn of Philippians 2:5–11 tells Christ's whole story. Though he was in the form of God, he didn't cling to his equal status, but emptied himself by taking the form of a servant and being born in human likeness. He humbled himself to death on a cross, so God highly exalted him and gave him the name above every name, so that every knee will bow and every tongue confess that "Jesus Christ is Lord" (echoing Isa 45:23).

For Paul, knowing Christ, sharing his sufferings, and attaining the resurrection is worth more than everything (3:8–11). We await him as Saviour from heaven, who will transform our lowly bodies to be like his glorious body (3:20–21).

## Common mix-ups

- "I can do all things through him who strengthens me" (4:13) is about contentment in every circumstance, plenty or want, not about achieving any goal we choose.
- Christ "emptying himself" (2:7) doesn't mean he stopped being God. He took on the form of a servant and laid aside his rights.
- Philippi was the first European city where Paul planted a church (Acts 16).
- "Work out your own salvation" (2:12) isn't earning salvation. God is at work in them (2:13).
- The letter's prison is most often thought to be Rome, but Ephesus and Caesarea have also been proposed.

## Going deeper

- **A letter of friendship and farewell.** Lowe reads Philippians in light of ancient farewells and Paul's possible death. How does that change how 1:21–26 and 2:17 read?
- **The Christ hymn (2:5–11).** What does it teach about Christ's deity, humanity, humiliation and exaltation? Why does Paul use it to address unity?
- **Loss and gain (3:4–11).** What were Paul's credentials, and why did he count them as rubbish? Connect 3:9 with justification by faith.
- **Anxiety and peace (4:4–9).** What does Paul prescribe instead of anxiety? How does the peace of God "guard" hearts and minds?
- **Contentment (4:10–13).** What is "the secret" Paul learned? How does it free us from both greed and despair?
- **Giving as partnership.** How does Paul describe the Philippians' gift (4:15–19)? What does that teach about supporting gospel work?

## Q&A

1. **Q:** Where was Paul when he wrote Philippians?

   **A:** In prison (1:7, 13).

2. **Q:** What is Paul sure God will do with the good work he began?

   **A:** Bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ (1:6).

3. **Q:** What did Paul say about living and dying?

   **A:** "To live is Christ, and to die is gain" (1:21).

4. **Q:** What did Christ do, according to 2:7–8?

   **A:** Emptied himself, took the form of a servant, and humbled himself to death on a cross.

5. **Q:** What will every tongue confess?

   **A:** That Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father (2:11).

6. **Q:** Who nearly died serving Paul?

   **A:** Epaphroditus (2:25–30).

7. **Q:** How did Paul regard his former credentials?

   **A:** As loss, even rubbish, compared with knowing Christ (3:7–8).

8. **Q:** Where is the believer's citizenship?

   **A:** In heaven (3:20).

9. **Q:** What should we do instead of being anxious?

   **A:** Pray with thanksgiving, and God's peace will guard our hearts (4:6–7).

10. **Q:** What does "I can do all things through him who strengthens me" refer to?

    **A:** Being content in every circumstance, in plenty and in need (4:11–13).
