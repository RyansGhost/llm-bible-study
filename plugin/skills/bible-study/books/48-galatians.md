# Galatians

## Summary

Galatians is Paul's urgent defence of the gospel of grace. False teachers have persuaded the Galatian believers that Gentile Christians must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to be truly right with God. Paul calls this "a different gospel" (1:6). He shows that a person is justified by faith in Christ, not by works of the law (2:16). Believers are Abraham's children and God's adopted sons by faith, and the freedom Christ gives is lived out by walking in the Spirit and serving one another in love. Luther loved Galatians so much he called it the letter he was "betrothed" to (quoted in Waters).

## Setting

- **When:** debated. Waters explains that the date depends on where "Galatia" was. On the "South Galatian" view, Paul wrote to the churches of his first journey (Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, Derbe), perhaps about AD 48–49, before the Jerusalem council. On the "North Galatian" view, Paul wrote to ethnic Galatians further north, about AD 54–55, during his third journey. The Reformation Study Bible notes that the later date puts it near Romans and 2 Corinthians, which share its concerns, but the earlier date fits Acts more easily: Paul's two Jerusalem visits in Galatians (1:18; 2:1–10) match Acts 9 and the famine visit of Acts 11:29–30, and his private meeting with the leaders doesn't look like the council of Acts 15.
- **Where:** the Roman province of Galatia, in central Asia Minor (modern Turkey).
- **Who wrote it:** Paul, "and all the brothers who are with me" (1:1–2). He wrote the closing in his own large letters (6:11).
- **Where it sits:** often called the "Magna Carta of Christian freedom". Its themes overlap with Romans. Waters notes we know Paul's opponents only through his responses: they apparently required circumcision and law-keeping of Gentile believers and questioned Paul's authority. The RSB finds three charges behind Paul's answers: that he had defied the Jerusalem apostles (1:11–2:10), that he had clashed with Peter (2:11–14), and that he once preached circumcision himself (5:11). Pressure from nationalist Jews in Judea may have driven them. Already the Galatians were deserting the gospel and quarrelling (1:6; 5:15). Unusually, Paul skips his normal thanksgiving and goes straight to rebuke.

## Structure

The Reformation Study Bible's outline: greeting (1:1–5), the problem of another gospel (1:6–9), Paul's defence of his apostleship (1:10–2:14), his defence of the gospel (2:15–6:10), and his conclusion in his own hand (6:11–18). Many see the shape of an ancient persuasive speech. Waters' outline of Paul's argument:

- 1:1–5: Greeting: Paul's apostleship comes from God; Christ gave himself to rescue us
- 1:6–10: The issue: the Galatians are turning to a different gospel
- 1:11–2:14: Paul's defence: his gospel and apostleship came by revelation, recognised in Jerusalem, and defended against Peter at Antioch
- 2:15–21: Thesis: justified by faith in Christ, not by works of the law
- 3:1–4:31: Argument: the Spirit, Abraham, the law's curse and purpose, adoption, and Hagar and Sarah
- 5:1–6:18: Appeal and application: freedom in Christ, walking by the Spirit, bearing burdens, and boasting only in the cross

## Chapter by chapter

- **Galatians 1:** Paul is an apostle through Jesus Christ and God the Father, not from men. He is astonished the Galatians are deserting to a different gospel: anyone preaching another gospel is accursed. He received his gospel by revelation. He had persecuted the church before God called him, and he didn't consult Jerusalem first.
- **Galatians 2:** Fourteen years later, the Jerusalem leaders recognised Paul's gospel and didn't compel Titus to be circumcised. At Antioch, Paul opposed Peter to his face for withdrawing from Gentile believers. We are justified by faith in Christ, not by works of the law (2:16). "I have been crucified with Christ. It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me" (2:20).
- **Galatians 3:** "O foolish Galatians!" Did you receive the Spirit by works of the law or by hearing with faith? Abraham believed God, and believers are his children. Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us (3:13). The law, given 430 years after the promise, can't cancel it: it was a guardian until Christ. In Christ "there is neither Jew nor Greek … slave nor free … male and female" (3:28).
- **Galatians 4:** When the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son to redeem those under the law, so we might be adopted as sons crying "Abba! Father!" Why return to slavery? Paul appeals as a parent in labour pains for them. The allegory of Hagar and Sarah: we are children of the free woman.
- **Galatians 5:** "For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm" (5:1). If you seek justification by the law you have fallen from grace. What counts is faith working through love. Walk by the Spirit: the works of the flesh against the fruit of the Spirit, "love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control" (5:22–23).
- **Galatians 6:** Restore one who sins, gently. Bear one another's burdens. You reap what you sow, so don't grow weary in doing good. Paul's large letters. He will boast only in the cross. What counts is a new creation, and he bears on his body the marks of Jesus.

## Key people

- **Paul:** the apostle defending the gospel of grace.
- **The false teachers ("Judaizers"):** who insisted Gentile believers be circumcised (1:7; 5:10–12; 6:12–13).
- **Peter (Cephas):** whom Paul rebuked at Antioch (2:11–14).
- **James and John:** "pillars" of the Jerusalem church (2:9).
- **Barnabas:** Paul's partner, who was also led astray at Antioch (2:13).
- **Titus:** a Greek believer not compelled to be circumcised (2:3).
- **Abraham:** the model of justification by faith (3:6–9).
- **Hagar and Sarah:** used as an allegory of two covenants (4:21–31).

## Key events

- Paul's conversion and call (1:13–16)
- Paul's visit to Peter in Jerusalem (1:18)
- The meeting with the Jerusalem pillars (2:1–10)
- Paul confronts Peter at Antioch (2:11–14)

## Themes

- **Justification by faith alone:** a person is declared righteous through faith in Christ, not by works of the law (2:16). Waters identifies 2:15–21 as the thesis of the letter.
- **One gospel:** there is no other gospel, and adding requirements to Christ destroys grace (1:6–9; 5:2–4).
- **Paul's apostolic authority:** his gospel came directly from Christ (1:11–12).
- **Promise and law:** the promise to Abraham came first. The law was added as a temporary guardian leading to Christ (3:15–25).
- **Adoption and the Spirit:** believers are God's sons and heirs, with the Spirit of his Son in their hearts (4:4–7). Paul mentions the Spirit even more often than justification: the Spirit is the blessing promised to Abraham (3:14), and the Galatians received him by faith, not law-keeping (3:2–3).
- **One gospel for all ages:** the gospel was preached beforehand to Abraham (3:8). All who belong to Christ, Abraham's true offspring, are Abraham's offspring too (3:16, 29). God has always had one way of salvation.
- **Freedom:** freedom from the law's curse and slavery, not freedom to sin, but to serve one another in love (5:1, 13).
- **Life in the Spirit:** the fruit of the Spirit against the works of the flesh (5:16–26).
- **The cross:** Christ bore the law's curse in our place (3:13); his death justifies and also frees us from sin (1:4; 2:20). The cross is Paul's only boast (6:14).
- **The "New Perspective" debate:** some recent scholars argue that "works of the law" means Jewish identity markers (circumcision, food laws, feasts), so that Galatians is about who belongs to the church rather than how a sinner is saved. The RSB answers that in 3:10–14 Paul plainly means deeds done to gain acceptance with God, and that the Reformation reading stands: justification by faith alone in Christ alone, which then defines the people of God.

## How it points to Christ

Galatians is a letter about Christ's sufficiency. He "gave himself for our sins to deliver us from the present evil age" (1:4). He loved me and gave himself for me (2:20). He redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us, hanging on a tree (3:13; Deut 21:23). He is the singular "offspring" of Abraham to whom the promises were made (3:16), and God's Son, born of a woman and born under the law, to redeem and adopt us (4:4–5). To add circumcision or law-keeping to him is to say "Christ died for no purpose" (2:21).

## Common mix-ups

- The "fruit of the Spirit" (5:22–23) is singular, one fruit with many qualities, not a list of separate gifts.
- Galatians was written to several churches across a region, not to one city.
- Paul's rebuke of Peter at Antioch (2:11–14) is only recorded in Galatians.
- "Neither Jew nor Greek … male and female" (3:28) is about equal standing in Christ as heirs of the promise. It doesn't erase all distinctions in every area of life.
- Galatians and Romans both teach justification by faith. Galatians is shorter, earlier and more urgent.
- "You reap what you sow" (6:7) is a Bible verse, from Galatians.

## Going deeper

- **Where was Galatia, and when was the letter written?** Waters sets out the North and South Galatian theories and how each affects the date. How does the answer connect with Acts 15?
- **Who were the opponents?** Waters reconstructs their teaching from Paul's replies. Why was requiring circumcision "a different gospel" rather than a minor addition?
- **Justification by faith (2:15–21).** How did this passage shape the Reformation? Compare Luther's Commentary on Galatians with Reformed teaching (WCF 11). Here Lutherans and Reformed agree.
- **What is the law for (3:19–25)?** If not for justification, why was it given? How does Paul's "guardian" image fit the Reformed "uses of the law"?
- **Freedom and the flesh (5).** How does walking by the Spirit avoid both legalism and licence?
- **Hagar and Sarah (4:21–31).** How does Paul use this "allegory"? What does it teach about two covenants?

## Q&A

1. Why was Paul astonished at the Galatians? They were quickly turning to a different gospel (1:6).
2. What did Paul say about anyone preaching another gospel? Let him be accursed (1:8–9).
3. How did Paul receive his gospel? Through a revelation of Jesus Christ, not from any man (1:12).
4. Whom did Paul confront at Antioch, and why? Peter, for withdrawing from eating with Gentile believers (2:11–14).
5. How is a person justified, according to 2:16? Through faith in Jesus Christ, not by works of the law.
6. What does Paul say in 2:20? "I have been crucified with Christ. It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me."
7. How did Christ redeem us from the curse of the law? By becoming a curse for us (3:13).
8. What did God send in the fullness of time? His Son, born of a woman, born under the law (4:4).
9. What is the fruit of the Spirit? Love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control (5:22–23).
10. In what alone will Paul boast? The cross of our Lord Jesus Christ (6:14).
