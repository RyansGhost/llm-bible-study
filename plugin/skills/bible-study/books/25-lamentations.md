# Lamentations

## Summary

Lamentations is five poems of grief over the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple by Babylon in 586 BC. They pour out the horror of the siege, confess that the disaster is God's just judgment on Judah's sin, and cry to God for mercy. At the very centre of the book's darkness shines its best-known confession: the Lord's steadfast love never ceases, and his mercies are new every morning (3:22–23).

## Setting

- **When:** just after Jerusalem fell in 586 BC (2 Kgs 25; Jer 39; 52).
- **Where:** the ruins of Jerusalem.
- **Who wrote it:** anonymous. Jewish and Christian tradition credits Jeremiah, who lamented and saw the fall (compare 2 Chr 35:25). Lee discusses this tradition.
- **Where it sits:** English Bibles, following the Greek and Latin, put it after Jeremiah. In the Hebrew Bible it is among the Writings, one of the five festival scrolls, read on the fast day mourning the temple's destruction (the 9th of Av).

## Structure

Five poems, one per chapter. Lee highlights the alphabetic acrostics: each stanza of chapters 1–4 begins with the next letter of the 22-letter Hebrew alphabet. Chapter 3 is a triple acrostic of 66 verses. Chapter 5 has 22 verses but isn't an acrostic, as if the order breaks down at the end. The acrostics give shape to overwhelming grief, expressing it "from A to Z".

- 1: The city's shame and mourning: Jerusalem as a lonely widow
- 2: The Lord's anger against Zion
- 3: One man's suffering, and hope in God's faithfulness
- 4: Then and now: the horrors of the siege
- 5: The community's prayer for restoration

## Chapter by chapter

- **Lamentations 1:** "How lonely sits the city": Jerusalem is like a widow, a fallen queen with no one to comfort her. Her sin brought this. She cries out: is there any sorrow like mine?
- **Lamentations 2:** The Lord in his anger has acted like an enemy, destroying Zion's walls, temple and festivals. False prophets had given empty visions, and children faint in the streets.
- **Lamentations 3:** A man who has seen affliction describes God's hand against him. Then the turn: "The steadfast love of the Lord never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness" (3:22–23). It is good to wait quietly for the Lord. Let us examine our ways and return to him.
- **Lamentations 4:** Then and now: gold grown dim, nobles starving, mothers driven to the unthinkable in famine, all because of the sins of the prophets and priests. Zion's punishment will end, and Edom's will come.
- **Lamentations 5:** A communal prayer: remember what has happened to us. "Restore us to yourself, O Lord, that we may be restored", unless you have utterly rejected us (5:21–22).

## Key people

- **The poet:** traditionally Jeremiah, speaking for the ruined city.
- **Lady Zion / Daughter of Jerusalem:** the city personified as a grieving woman.
- **The man of affliction (3):** an individual sufferer who represents the people and models turning to God.
- **The Lord:** the one who has judged, and the only one who can restore.
- **Edom:** a neighbour that gloated over Jerusalem's fall (4:21–22).

## Key events

- The siege and fall of Jerusalem (586 BC)
- The temple destroyed and worship ended (2:6–7)
- Famine in the siege (2:11–12; 4:3–10)
- The exile of the people (1:3)

## Themes

- **Grief brought honestly to God:** Lamentations gives words for overwhelming sorrow and shows that lament is part of faith.
- **God's righteous judgment:** the destruction is the Lord's doing, and it is just: "The Lord is in the right, for I have rebelled" (1:18). The covenant curses have fallen (Deut 28).
- **Sin and confession:** the poems confess sin, especially of the prophets and priests (2:14; 4:13).
- **Hope in God's faithfulness:** in the centre of the book, hope rests on God's character, his steadfast love and mercy (3:21–33).
- **Waiting on God:** "It is good that one should wait quietly for the salvation of the Lord" (3:26).
- **An unfinished prayer:** the book ends with a plea, not a resolution (5:21–22), leaving God's people waiting for restoration.

## How it points to Christ

Jesus also wept over Jerusalem and foretold its destruction (Luke 19:41–44). The cry of Lady Zion, "Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by? Look and see if there is any sorrow like my sorrow" (1:12), has long been applied to Christ on the cross. There the full wrath of God against sin fell, not on a guilty city but on the innocent Son, in place of his people.

Because of him, the mercies of 3:22–23 are secured for good: in Christ, God's steadfast love truly never ceases (Rom 8:38–39). The unanswered prayer of 5:21, "restore us", is answered in the new Jerusalem, where God wipes away every tear (Rev 21:2–4).

## Common mix-ups

- "Great is your faithfulness" comes from Lamentations 3:23, not the Psalms.
- Lamentations is not part of Jeremiah, though English Bibles place it next to it and tradition links them.
- The book mourns Jerusalem's destruction by Babylon (586 BC), not by the Romans (AD 70).
- Chapters 1–4 are acrostics; chapter 5 is not.
- The book is poetry, not narrative. The history is in 2 Kings 25 and Jeremiah 39 and 52.

## Going deeper

- **Why acrostics?** Lee explains the acrostic structure of chapters 1–4 and the near-acrostic shape of chapter 5. Why would grief be written in such an ordered form? Read: Lee, Structure and Outline.
- **Jerusalem as a woman.** Lee traces the personification: widow, queen, abandoned lover. How does it make the loss personal?
- **Hope at the centre (3:21–33).** Why does hope appear in the middle of the book rather than at the end? What is that hope based on?
- **Is God's anger compatible with his love?** Lamentations says both that God has acted like an enemy (2:5) and that his mercies never end (3:22). How do these fit together?
- **Lament in the church.** The book gives believers words for grief, both personal and communal. When might a congregation pray Lamentations?

## Q&A

1. What event does Lamentations mourn? The destruction of Jerusalem and the temple by Babylon in 586 BC.
2. What does the Hebrew title mean? "How!", from the opening word of chapters 1, 2 and 4.
3. Who is traditionally believed to have written it? Jeremiah.
4. How is Jerusalem pictured in chapter 1? As a lonely widow with no one to comfort her.
5. What is special about the structure of chapters 1–4? They are acrostics, following the Hebrew alphabet.
6. How many verses does chapter 3 have, and why? 66: a triple acrostic, three verses for each of the 22 letters.
7. What famous words of hope are in chapter 3? "The steadfast love of the Lord never ceases … his mercies are new every morning; great is your faithfulness" (3:22–23).
8. Whom does the book blame for Jerusalem's fall? The people's sin, especially of the prophets and priests; God's judgment was just (1:18; 4:13).
9. Which neighbour is singled out for gloating? Edom (4:21–22).
10. How does the book end? With a prayer for God to restore his people (5:21–22).
