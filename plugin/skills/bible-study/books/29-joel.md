## Summary

A devastating locust plague strips Judah bare, and Joel reads it as a warning of the coming "day of the Lord". He calls the whole nation to fast, weep and return to God with torn hearts, not just torn clothes. God promises to restore what the locusts ate, to pour out his Spirit on all people, and finally to judge the nations and dwell in Zion with his people.

## Setting

- **When:** uncertain. The book gives no king's name. The Lutheran Study Bible places it after Jehoshaphat's reign (9th century BC) or later; many scholars prefer after the exile.
- **Where:** Judah and Jerusalem, with its temple, priests and elders.
- **Who wrote it:** Joel son of Pethuel (1:1). Nothing else is known about him.
- **Where it sits:** second of the Twelve in the Hebrew order. Timmer sees the day of the Lord as the overarching theme of the Twelve, and Joel is full of it.

## Structure

- 1:1–2:17: The locust plague and the coming day of the Lord: a call to lament and repent
- 2:18–27: God's answer: pity, and the land restored
- 2:28–32: The Spirit poured out on all flesh
- 3: The nations judged in the Valley of Jehoshaphat, and the Lord dwelling in Zion

## Chapter by chapter

- **Joel 1:** Wave after wave of locusts destroys the crops, vines and trees. Joel calls drunkards, farmers and priests to mourn, and the elders to call a fast, for the day of the Lord is near.
- **Joel 2:** The locusts are like an invading army, and the day of the Lord is great and terrible. "Return to me with all your heart … rend your hearts and not your garments", for God is gracious and merciful (2:12–13). God takes pity: "I will restore to you the years that the swarming locust has eaten" (2:25). Afterwards he will pour out his Spirit on all flesh, with wonders in the heavens, and everyone who calls on the Lord's name will be saved (2:28–32).
- **Joel 3:** God gathers the nations to the Valley of Jehoshaphat for judgment: "Beat your plowshares into swords". The Lord roars from Zion, the mountains drip with wine, and the Lord dwells in Zion.

## Key people

- **Joel son of Pethuel:** the prophet.
- **The priests and elders:** called to lead the nation in fasting and lament (1:13–14; 2:15–17).
- **The nations:** judged for scattering God's people and dividing his land (3:2).

## Key events

- The locust plague (1)
- The call to a solemn assembly and fast (1:14; 2:15)
- The promise of restoration (2:18–27)
- The promise of the Spirit (2:28–32)
- The judgment in the Valley of Jehoshaphat (3)

## Themes

- **The day of the Lord:** a day of darkness and judgment for the unrepentant, and salvation for those who call on God (1:15; 2:1–11, 31–32; 3:14). Timmer describes it as the "metatheme" of the Twelve.
- **True repentance:** inward, wholehearted turning, "rend your hearts" (2:12–13). Timmer notes that repentance doesn't guarantee escape from every judgment ("who knows whether he will turn", 2:14), but it is the way back to God.
- **God's character:** "gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love" (2:13; Ex 34:6).
- **Restoration:** God gives back more than was lost (2:23–27).
- **The Spirit for all God's people:** not just prophets, kings and priests, but sons and daughters, old and young, servants (2:28–29).
- **Judgment on the nations, safety in Zion:** God judges those who oppressed his people, and is a refuge for them (3:16).

## How it points to Christ

On the day of Pentecost, Peter declared that Joel's promise of the Spirit poured out on all flesh had been fulfilled through the risen and exalted Jesus (2:28–32; Acts 2:16–21, 33). Paul quotes "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (2:32) for salvation in Christ, for Jew and Gentile alike (Rom 10:13). The "day of the Lord" reaches its climax at Christ's return (2 Pet 3:10; Rev 6:12–17).

## Common mix-ups

- Joel's date is unknown and debated; don't assume it's the earliest prophet.
- The "day of the Lord" isn't a 24-hour day but a time of God's decisive intervention in judgment and salvation.
- Joel 3:10 ("beat your plowshares into swords") deliberately reverses Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3.
- In some Bibles Joel has four chapters: the English 2:28–32 is Hebrew 3:1–5, and English 3 is Hebrew 4.
- Peter's Pentecost sermon (Acts 2) quotes Joel, not Isaiah.

## Going deeper

- **The locusts: literal or symbolic?** Is chapter 2's army the same locusts, a human army pictured as locusts, or the day of the Lord itself?
- **"Who knows whether he will not turn?" (2:14).** Timmer notes that repentance doesn't guarantee deliverance from every historical judgment, though it is linked to true life. How does that humility shape our prayers?
- **The Spirit on all flesh.** What was new compared with the Spirit's work in Moses' day (Num 11:29)? How does Acts 2 interpret it?
- **"I will restore the years the locusts have eaten."** How has this promise comforted believers after wasted or painful years? What are its limits?
- **The nations in the Twelve.** Timmer shows that "the nations" are judged when they resist God, yet some are included when they turn to him. How does Joel 2:32 ("everyone who calls") open the door?

## Q&A

1. What disaster struck Judah in Joel? A plague of locusts (1:4).
2. What did Joel call the people to do? Fast, weep, and return to the Lord with all their heart (1:14; 2:12).
3. What should be torn, according to 2:13? Their hearts, not their garments.
4. How is God described in 2:13? Gracious and merciful, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love.
5. What did God promise to restore? The years the locusts had eaten (2:25).
6. On whom will God pour out his Spirit? On all flesh: sons and daughters, old and young, male and female servants (2:28–29).
7. Who will be saved on the day of the Lord? Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord (2:32).
8. Where are the nations judged? In the Valley of Jehoshaphat (3:2, 12).
9. Who quoted Joel on the day of Pentecost? Peter (Acts 2:16–21).
10. How does Joel end? With the promise that the Lord dwells in Zion (3:21).
