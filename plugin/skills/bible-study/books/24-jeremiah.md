# Jeremiah

## Summary

Jeremiah prophesied through Judah's last forty years, warning a stubborn people to repent or face Babylon. They rejected, beat, imprisoned and nearly killed him, and Jerusalem fell exactly as he said. Yet in the darkest hour, God gave Jeremiah the promise of a new covenant: God would write his law on his people's hearts and forgive their sins (31:31–34). Lee sums up the movement of his message as "from repentance to redemption".

## Setting

- **When:** from his call in 626 BC (the Reformation Heritage KJV Study Bible, RH, says 627), Josiah's thirteenth year, a ministry of over forty years through the reigns of Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin and Zedekiah, to the fall of Jerusalem (586 BC) and the flight to Egypt (1:2–3; 43–44). Josiah's reforms (from 628 BC, renewed when the Book of the Law was found in 621 BC) didn't change the people's hearts, and Manasseh's idolatry returned under his successors (Reformation Study Bible). Lee shows how this spans the collapse of Assyria (Nineveh fell in 612 BC) and the rise of Babylon, which took captives in 605, 597 and 586 BC.
- **Where:** Jerusalem and Judah; Anathoth, his priestly hometown in Benjamin; finally Egypt, where he probably died.
- **Who wrote it:** Jeremiah son of Hilkiah, a priest, whose name means "the Lord exalts" (or, RH suggests, "the Lord appoints"). His scribe Baruch wrote the first edition in 605 BC; King Jehoiakim burned it, and it was rewritten and later expanded (36). Chapter 52 is a later postscript, since 51:64 marks "the end of the words of Jeremiah"; its last event is Jehoiachin's release (about 561 BC). Lee calls it an anthology of oracles in many forms, gathered over time.
- **Where it sits:** the second Major Prophet. Its history overlaps 2 Kings 22–25. The Greek Old Testament has a shorter version (about an eighth shorter) in a different order, perhaps reflecting the book's growth in stages (Lee; RSB).

## Structure

The prophecies aren't in date order, and the same event can appear far apart (7 and 26). Lee notes that even John Bright called it a seeming "hodgepodge". The Reformation Study Bible's outline:

- 1: Jeremiah's call
- 2–20: Oracles mainly of judgment on Judah: the covenant lawsuit, the temple sermon, sign-acts, and Jeremiah's "confessions"
- 21–24: The end of David's dynasty; salvation only through exile
- 25–29: Babylon must rule the nations: the seventy years, the clash with false prophets, the letter to the exiles
- 30–33: Promises of restoration (the "Book of Consolation"): the new covenant
- 34–39: Jerusalem's last days: the burned scroll, Jeremiah imprisoned, the city falls
- 40–45: After the fall: Gedaliah's murder and the flight to Egypt
- 46–51: Oracles against the nations, ending with Babylon
- 52: Appendix: the fall of Jerusalem

## Chapter by chapter

- **Jeremiah 1:** God calls the young Jeremiah: "Before I formed you in the womb I knew you" (1:5). He puts his words in Jeremiah's mouth and shows him an almond branch (God is watching over his word) and a boiling pot tilted from the north.
- **Jeremiah 2:** Israel has forsaken the fountain of living waters for broken cisterns (2:13).
- **Jeremiah 3:** Faithless Israel and treacherous Judah are called to return to the Lord.
- **Jeremiah 4:** Circumcise your hearts. Disaster comes from the north, and Jeremiah writhes in anguish.
- **Jeremiah 5:** Search Jerusalem for one just person. The people refuse correction.
- **Jeremiah 6:** A siege is coming. False leaders cry "Peace, peace" when there is no peace (6:14). Ask for the ancient paths (6:16).
- **Jeremiah 7:** The temple sermon: don't trust in "the temple of the Lord" while living wickedly. Remember Shiloh.
- **Jeremiah 8:** "The harvest is past … and we are not saved" (8:20). Is there no balm in Gilead?
- **Jeremiah 9:** The weeping prophet mourns. Let the one who boasts boast in knowing the Lord (9:23–24).
- **Jeremiah 10:** Idols are scarecrows in a cucumber field; the Lord is the true and living God.
- **Jeremiah 11:** The covenant is broken. Men of Anathoth plot to kill Jeremiah.
- **Jeremiah 12:** Jeremiah asks why the wicked prosper; God warns that worse is coming.
- **Jeremiah 13:** A ruined linen belt pictures Judah's pride. "Can the leopard change his spots?" (13:23).
- **Jeremiah 14:** Drought. False prophets promise peace, and Jeremiah is told not to pray for the people.
- **Jeremiah 15:** Even Moses and Samuel couldn't turn God's judgment. Jeremiah complains, yet God's words were his joy (15:16).
- **Jeremiah 16:** Jeremiah is not to marry, as a sign of coming disaster. Yet a new exodus from the north is promised.
- **Jeremiah 17:** Blessed is the one who trusts in the Lord, like a tree by water (17:7–8). The heart is deceitful above all things (17:9). Keep the Sabbath.
- **Jeremiah 18:** At the potter's house: God can reshape nations as a potter reshapes clay.
- **Jeremiah 19:** Jeremiah smashes a clay jar at Topheth as a sign of Jerusalem's destruction.
- **Jeremiah 20:** Pashhur the priest puts him in the stocks. Jeremiah laments, yet God's word is like fire shut up in his bones (20:9).
- **Jeremiah 21:** Zedekiah asks for help; the answer is that Jerusalem will fall, and those who surrender will live.
- **Jeremiah 22:** Oracles against Judah's kings: Shallum, Jehoiakim, and Coniah (Jehoiachin), none of whose sons will sit on David's throne.
- **Jeremiah 23:** Woe to the shepherds who scatter the flock. God will raise a righteous Branch for David: "The Lord is our righteousness" (23:5–6). False prophets condemned.
- **Jeremiah 24:** Two baskets of figs: the exiles are the good figs, those who stay are the bad.
- **Jeremiah 25:** Seventy years of Babylonian rule (25:11), and the cup of God's wrath for the nations.
- **Jeremiah 26:** After the temple sermon Jeremiah is tried for his life and spared. The prophet Uriah is killed.
- **Jeremiah 27:** Jeremiah wears a yoke: submit to Nebuchadnezzar.
- **Jeremiah 28:** The false prophet Hananiah breaks the yoke and promises a quick return. He dies within the year.
- **Jeremiah 29:** A letter to the exiles: build houses, seek the city's welfare. After seventy years, God's plans for their welfare and a future (29:11).
- **Jeremiah 30:** The Book of Consolation begins: restoration after a time of distress.
- **Jeremiah 31:** Rachel weeps for her children (31:15). The new covenant: the law written on hearts, and sins remembered no more (31:31–34).
- **Jeremiah 32:** During the siege, Jeremiah buys a field at Anathoth, a sign that land will be bought again.
- **Jeremiah 33:** Restoration promised. The righteous Branch, and God's covenants with David and the Levites as sure as day and night.
- **Jeremiah 34:** Zedekiah's fate. The people free their slaves and then take them back.
- **Jeremiah 35:** The faithful Rechabites shame Judah's unfaithfulness.
- **Jeremiah 36:** Baruch writes Jeremiah's words on a scroll. King Jehoiakim cuts it up and burns it, and it is written again.
- **Jeremiah 37:** Jeremiah is arrested as a deserter and imprisoned. Zedekiah consults him secretly.
- **Jeremiah 38:** Thrown into a muddy cistern, Jeremiah is rescued by Ebed-melech the Cushite.
- **Jeremiah 39:** Jerusalem falls. Zedekiah is blinded and taken to Babylon. Jeremiah is freed, and Ebed-melech is promised safety.
- **Jeremiah 40:** Jeremiah chooses to stay in the land with Governor Gedaliah at Mizpah.
- **Jeremiah 41:** Ishmael murders Gedaliah.
- **Jeremiah 42:** The survivors ask for God's will. Through Jeremiah he tells them to stay in the land and not go to Egypt.
- **Jeremiah 43:** They go to Egypt anyway, taking Jeremiah and Baruch with them.
- **Jeremiah 44:** The Jews in Egypt worship the "queen of heaven", and judgment is pronounced.
- **Jeremiah 45:** A word to Baruch: don't seek great things for yourself; your life will be spared.
- **Jeremiah 46:** Oracle against Egypt, set at the battle of Carchemish (605 BC).
- **Jeremiah 47:** Oracle against the Philistines.
- **Jeremiah 48:** Oracle against Moab.
- **Jeremiah 49:** Oracles against Ammon, Edom, Damascus, Kedar and Elam.
- **Jeremiah 50:** Babylon itself will fall, and Israel will return.
- **Jeremiah 51:** More on Babylon's fall. A scroll of these words is sunk in the Euphrates.
- **Jeremiah 52:** The fall of Jerusalem retold, ending with Jehoiachin's release in Babylon.

## Key people

- **Jeremiah:** a priest's son from Anathoth, called as a youth; "the weeping prophet". God forbade him to marry, as a sign that normal life was about to end (16:2).
- **Baruch:** Jeremiah's scribe and companion.
- **Josiah:** the reforming king in whose reign Jeremiah was called.
- **Jehoiakim:** the king who burned Jeremiah's scroll.
- **Jehoiachin (Coniah, Jeconiah):** the king exiled in 597 BC.
- **Zedekiah:** Judah's last king, weak and wavering.
- **Nebuchadnezzar:** king of Babylon, called God's servant for judgment (25:9).
- **Hananiah:** a false prophet who contradicted Jeremiah.
- **Pashhur:** the priest who put Jeremiah in the stocks.
- **Ebed-melech:** the Cushite official who rescued Jeremiah from the cistern.
- **Gedaliah:** the governor after the fall, murdered by Ishmael.
- **The Rechabites:** a family faithful to their ancestor's commands.

## Themes

- **Covenant lawsuit:** Lee shows Jeremiah prosecuting God's case against Judah for breaking the Mosaic covenant: "I still contend with you" (2:9).
- **Repent or face judgment:** Lee traces a shift. From 626 to 597 BC Jeremiah calls for repentance; after 597 he announces judgment as certain, the covenant's final curse of losing the land (Deut 28). Then God would save a remnant through exile, and Babylon itself would fall after seventy years (25:11–12), as it did to Cyrus in 539 BC (RSB).
- **The deceitful heart:** sin goes deeper than outward acts; the heart itself is sick (17:9). That is why reform fails and a new covenant is needed.
- **False security:** trusting in the temple, alliances or prophets who say "peace" when there is none (6:14; 7:4).
- **The cost of faithfulness:** Jeremiah's "confessions" (11:18–20:18) show a prophet suffering, lonely and wrestling with God, even accusing him (20:7). He feels both the people's coming agony and God's grief over their sin. RH compares him with Moses, who also protested his call (1:6; Ex 4:10), and with Paul, set apart before birth and rejected by his own nation (1:5; Gal 1:15), but notes that Jeremiah, the "weeping prophet", had almost no friends and saw little fruit.
- **True and false prophets:** popular prophets promised peace and a quick return; Jeremiah, sent from the Lord's council, told the hard truth (23:16–22; 28).
- **God's sovereignty over nations:** the potter and the clay (18). Babylon serves God's purposes, then is judged.
- **Hope beyond judgment:** a righteous Branch from David, called "The Lord is our righteousness" (23:5–6), a return after seventy years (29:10), and a new covenant in which God enables his people to know and obey him (31:31–34; 32:40). Hebrews shows it fulfilled in Christ (Heb 8).

## How it points to Christ

The new covenant promised in Jeremiah 31:31–34 is fulfilled in Jesus: "This cup … is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20). Hebrews quotes the whole promise and says Christ is its mediator (Heb 8:6–13; 10:15–18). The righteous Branch who will be called "The Lord is our righteousness" (23:5–6) is the Son of David who becomes our righteousness (1 Cor 1:30).

Jesus quoted Jeremiah's temple sermon when he cleared the temple: "a den of robbers" (7:11; Matt 21:13). Matthew links Rachel's weeping to Herod's slaughter of the infants (31:15; Matt 2:17–18). Some people even thought Jesus was Jeremiah (Matt 16:14). Like Jeremiah, Jesus wept over Jerusalem and was rejected by its leaders (Luke 19:41–44). RH sees Jeremiah himself as a picture of the suffering servant: no one but Christ was treated with more contempt by Israel.

## Common mix-ups

- Jeremiah was not taken to Babylon. He stayed in Judah and was later taken to Egypt (43).
- Baruch was his scribe, not a prophet in his own right (though a later apocryphal book bears his name).
- Jehoiachin, Coniah and Jeconiah are the same king.
- Hananiah in Jeremiah 28 is a false prophet, not Daniel's friend Hananiah (Shadrach).
- Jeremiah's famous "plans to prosper you" (29:11) was spoken to exiles told to wait seventy years, not a promise of quick success.
- The new covenant (31:31–34) is a Jeremiah text; Hebrews 8 quotes it in full.
- Lamentations is traditionally linked with Jeremiah, but it is a separate book.

## Going deeper

- **Two versions of Jeremiah.** Lee explains that the Greek and Hebrew versions differ in length and order, partly confirmed by the Dead Sea Scrolls. How should we think about this, and does it affect our confidence in Scripture? Read: Lee, Text of Jeremiah.
- **From repentance to redemption.** Lee, following Unterman, traces Jeremiah's message moving from calls to repent to announcements of judgment and future redemption. Map chapters 3, 7, 21, 29 and 31 along it.
- **Jeremiah's confessions.** Read his laments (11:18–12:6; 15:10–21; 17:14–18; 18:18–23; 20:7–18). What do they teach about honest prayer under suffering?
- **What's new about the new covenant?** Compare 31:31–34 with the Sinai covenant. Is the newness forgiveness, the inner law, universal knowledge of God, or all three? How does Hebrews 8 apply it?
- **True and false prophets.** Compare Jeremiah and Hananiah (27–28) with Deuteronomy 18:15–22, which Lee shows Jeremiah's call alludes to. How can God's people tell them apart?
- **Seeking the city's welfare (29:4–7).** How does this letter to exiles shape Christians living in a culture that isn't their home (1 Pet 2:11–12)?

## Q&A

1. **Q:** When did God say he knew Jeremiah?

   **A:** Before he formed him in the womb (1:5).

2. **Q:** What two images did God show Jeremiah at his call?

   **A:** An almond branch and a boiling pot facing from the north (1:11–14).

3. **Q:** What did the people trade the fountain of living waters for?

   **A:** Broken cisterns that hold no water (2:13).

4. **Q:** What did false prophets say when there was no peace?

   **A:** "Peace, peace" (6:14; 8:11).

5. **Q:** What lesson did Jeremiah learn at the potter's house?

   **A:** God can reshape nations as the potter reshapes clay (18:1–10).

6. **Q:** How long would Babylon rule, according to Jeremiah?

   **A:** Seventy years (25:11; 29:10).

7. **Q:** What did God promise in the new covenant?

   **A:** To put his law in their hearts, to be their God, and to forgive their sin (31:31–34).

8. **Q:** Who wrote down Jeremiah's words, and which king burned the scroll?

   **A:** Baruch wrote them; King Jehoiakim burned the scroll (36:4, 23).

9. **Q:** Who rescued Jeremiah from the cistern?

   **A:** Ebed-melech the Cushite (38:7–13).

10. **Q:** Where did Jeremiah end up after Jerusalem fell?

    **A:** In Egypt, taken by the fleeing Jews (43:4–7).
