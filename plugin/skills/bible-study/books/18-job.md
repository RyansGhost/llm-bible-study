# Job

## Summary

Job is about a righteous man who loses everything and wrestles with God over why. Behind the scenes, God allows Satan to test whether Job serves God for nothing. Job's friends insist he must be suffering for some sin. Job insists on his innocence and demands a hearing with God. When God finally speaks from the whirlwind, he doesn't explain Job's suffering. He reveals his own wisdom and power, and Job bows in humble trust. God rebukes the friends and restores Job.

## Setting

- **When:** the events seem to belong to the age of the patriarchs (about 2000–1700 BC). Belcher[^bto] points to wealth counted in livestock, Job offering sacrifices as head of his family, and his long life of 140 years. The date of writing is unknown: the *Reformation Study Bible*[^rsb] places the author after 1500 BC, perhaps as late as Solomon's time, when wisdom writing flourished. Ezekiel's mention of Job (Ezek 14:14, 20) and copies among the Dead Sea Scrolls rule out a very late date. The *Reformation Heritage KJV Study Bible* (*RH*[^rh]) sets the events even earlier, about 2500–2000 BC, noting how often God is called "the Almighty" (*Shaddai*), the name linked with the patriarchs (Ex 6:3). It suggests Solomon's reign, the high point of wisdom writing, as a likely time of writing, perhaps by Solomon himself.
- **Where:** the land of Uz, east of Canaan, probably around Edom or north Arabia. Job is not an Israelite, and the book never mentions the law of Moses.
- **Who wrote it:** unknown. Not Job himself, the *RSB*[^rsb] argues: if he had known about the scene in heaven, the whole debate would have been pointless. The author was an Israelite poet, since the narrator uses God's covenant name, the LORD, while Job and his friends almost never do; he probably drew on older sources from Job's time. The *Lutheran Study Bible*[^lsb] suggests Job or someone of his time.
- **Where it sits:** the first of the Wisdom and Poetry books. Belcher[^bto] compares it with Egyptian and Babylonian texts about the suffering of the righteous, and notes how differently Job answers. Its poetry blends lawsuit, lament and debate, and its Hebrew is among the hardest in the Bible.

## Structure

Belcher's[^bto] outline. The prologue and epilogue are prose; everything between is poetry. The *RSB*[^rsb] groups it as prologue (1–2), dialogue (3–27), monologues (28:1–42:6: wisdom poem, Job, Elihu, the Lord), and epilogue (42:7–17).

- 1–2: Prologue: Job's character, the scenes in heaven, and the two tests
- 3: Job's lament
- 4–27: The cycles of speeches: Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar, with Job's replies (the third cycle breaks off incomplete)
- 28: A poem on wisdom
- 29–31: Job's last defence
- 32–37: Elihu's speeches
- 38:1–42:6: God's speeches from the whirlwind, and Job's response
- 42:7–17: Epilogue: the friends rebuked and Job restored

*RH*[^rh] reads the book as built in matching layers around the wisdom poem of chapter 28: prose at both ends, Job's lament (3) balanced by his confession (42:1–6), and three rounds of dialogue balanced by three sets of speeches (Job, Elihu, the Lord). At the centre stands "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom" (28:28).

## Chapter by chapter

Job is mostly speeches, so these are short summaries of each speaker's argument.

- **Job 1:** Job, blameless and wealthy, fears God. In heaven Satan claims Job serves God only for what he gets, and God permits a test. Job loses his flocks, servants and ten children, yet worships: "the Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away" (1:21).
- **Job 2:** In a second test Job is covered in painful sores. His wife says "curse God and die", but he doesn't sin with his lips. Three friends come and sit with him in silence for seven days.
- **Job 3:** Job breaks his silence and curses the day he was born, wishing he had never lived.
- **Job 4:** Eliphaz: the innocent don't perish. He describes a night vision: no mortal is righteous before God.
- **Job 5:** Eliphaz: turn to God, and receive his discipline as a blessing.
- **Job 6:** Job: my anguish is crushing, and you friends are like streams that dry up when needed.
- **Job 7:** Job: life is a breath. Why does God watch me so closely and not leave me alone?
- **Job 8:** Bildad: God doesn't twist justice. Your children must have sinned. Seek God and he will restore you.
- **Job 9:** Job: how can anyone be right before God? There is no arbiter to stand between us (9:33).
- **Job 10:** Job: why did you make me, only to destroy me? Leave me alone a little.
- **Job 11:** Zophar: you deserve even worse. Put away your sin.
- **Job 12:** Job: I'm not inferior to you. Everyone knows God's power rules all things.
- **Job 13:** Job: I want to argue my case with God. "Though he slay me, I will hope in him" (13:15).
- **Job 14:** Job: man is like a fading flower. "If a man dies, shall he live again?" (14:14).
- **Job 15:** Eliphaz: your own words condemn you. The wicked live in torment.
- **Job 16:** Job: you are miserable comforters. Yet even now my witness is in heaven (16:19).
- **Job 17:** Job: my spirit is broken, and my hope has gone down to the grave.
- **Job 18:** Bildad: the terrible end of the wicked.
- **Job 19:** Job: my friends torment me and God has stripped me. Yet "I know that my Redeemer lives", and in my flesh I will see God (19:25–27).
- **Job 20:** Zophar: the joy of the wicked is brief.
- **Job 21:** Job: but the wicked often prosper and die in peace. Your theory doesn't fit the facts.
- **Job 22:** Eliphaz accuses Job of specific sins against the poor, and urges him to return to God.
- **Job 23:** Job: if only I knew where to find God! When he has tested me, I will come out as gold (23:10).
- **Job 24:** Job: God seems not to judge the oppressors.
- **Job 25:** Bildad, briefly: how can a man be pure before God?
- **Job 26:** Job: God's majesty; creation is only the outskirts of his ways.
- **Job 27:** Job holds on to his integrity, and describes the end of the wicked.
- **Job 28:** A poem: people can mine treasures from the earth, but where can wisdom be found? "The fear of the Lord, that is wisdom" (28:28).
- **Job 29:** Job remembers his former blessed and honoured days.
- **Job 30:** Job laments his present humiliation and suffering.
- **Job 31:** Job's oath of innocence, listing sins he has not committed, and he signs his defence.
- **Job 32:** Elihu, a young man, angry at both Job and the friends, begins to speak.
- **Job 33:** Elihu: God speaks through dreams and through suffering, to turn people back from the pit.
- **Job 34:** Elihu: far be it from God to do wrong.
- **Job 35:** Elihu: your righteousness doesn't put God in your debt.
- **Job 36:** Elihu: God disciplines in order to teach.
- **Job 37:** Elihu: God's majesty in the thunderstorm.
- **Job 38:** The Lord answers Job out of the whirlwind: "Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth?" (38:4). He questions Job about creation and the weather.
- **Job 39:** God's care for wild animals: mountain goats, the wild donkey, the ostrich, the war horse, the hawk.
- **Job 40:** Job puts his hand over his mouth. God speaks again and describes Behemoth.
- **Job 41:** Leviathan, a creature no one can tame.
- **Job 42:** Job repents in dust and ashes. God says the friends haven't spoken rightly as Job has, and Job prays for them. God restores Job's fortunes twice over.

## Key people

- **Job:** a blameless, God-fearing man of Uz, tested by terrible suffering.
- **Satan ("the accuser"):** claims Job's faith is self-interested, and is allowed to test him within limits God sets.
- **Job's wife:** urges him to curse God and die (2:9).
- **Eliphaz the Temanite:** the eldest friend, relying on experience and a vision.
- **Bildad the Shuhite:** a friend who appeals to tradition.
- **Zophar the Naamathite:** the harshest friend.
- **Elihu:** a younger man who speaks after the friends fall silent.
- **The Lord:** who speaks from the whirlwind.

## Themes

- **Suffering of the righteous:** Job shows that suffering isn't always punishment for particular sins. It's true that God punishes the wicked and rewards the faithful, but not on our timetable, and great suffering doesn't prove great sin (Luke 13:1–5; John 9:1–3). The friends run that rule in reverse (21; 42:7). Job suffers not because he is one of the worst but one of the best (*RSB*[^rsb]). Belcher[^bto] shows how the prologue gives readers the view Job lacks.
- **Does anyone serve God for nothing?** The test in the prologue (1:9) asks whether faith is real or only a bargain for blessings.
- **God's sovereignty over Satan and suffering:** Satan can do nothing without God's permission, and God sets the limits (1:12; 2:6).
- **Honest lament:** Job pours out his complaints to God, not just about him; the friends say true things about God but never speak to him. God says Job spoke rightly, and the friends did not (42:7–8). Yet Job goes too far in questioning God's justice, and repents (40:8; 42:6).
- **God's wisdom beyond ours:** God never tells Job about the heavenly test. He shows his wisdom in creation, and Job learns to trust him without full answers (38–42).
- **The longing for a mediator:** Job longs for an arbiter, a witness in heaven, and a Redeemer (9:33; 16:19–21; 19:25).

## How it points to Christ

Job's cries for someone to stand between him and God (9:33), a witness in heaven to plead for him (16:19–21), and a living Redeemer he will see in his flesh (19:25–27) are answered in Jesus. Job also asks God to be his "surety" (17:3, KJV), and Elihu speaks of a mediator who finds a ransom so that a sinner is spared from death (33:23–28) (*RH*[^rh]). He is the one mediator between God and humanity (1 Tim 2:5), our advocate with the Father (1 John 2:1), and the risen Redeemer whose resurrection guarantees ours.

Job is a righteous sufferer, though not sinless. Christ is the truly innocent sufferer, who endured far more and entrusted himself to God (1 Pet 2:22–23). As Job prays for the friends who wronged him (42:8–10), Christ intercedes for sinners. Job's path, humbled while God seemed hidden and then raised up, follows the shape of Christ's suffering and glory (*RH*[^rh]). Satan the accuser, active in Job's story, is finally thrown down by the Lamb (Rev 12:10–11).

## Common mix-ups

- Job's suffering was not punishment for his sin. God called him blameless (1:8) and said the friends were wrong (42:7).
- "The patience of Job" comes from James 5:11, which is better translated "steadfastness". Job complained bitterly, but never let go of God.
- Satan in Job is "the accuser" acting only with God's permission, not an equal rival to God.
- Elihu is not one of the three friends; he speaks after they finish.
- Job is not an Israelite, and the book never mentions the law, the temple or the exodus.
- God never tells Job why he suffered. The reader knows about the heavenly test; Job doesn't.
- Job received twice as much as before, but the same number of children: ten.

## Going deeper

- **The friends' theology.** Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar say many true things about God, yet God condemns them (42:7). Where did they go wrong? Belcher[^bto] traces their insistence that Job must be guilty. Read: Belcher[^bto], The Speeches of the Friends.
- **What does God's answer mean?** God answers with questions about creation, not explanations (38–41). Why does this satisfy Job? What do Behemoth and Leviathan represent?
- **The incomplete third cycle.** Zophar never gives a third speech. Belcher[^bto] argues the breakdown is deliberate: the friends have run out of answers.
- **"I know that my Redeemer lives" (19:25–27).** What did Job mean, and how far did he see? How do Christians rightly read it in light of Christ and the resurrection?
- **Is it right to lament?** Job curses his birthday and accuses God of injustice, yet God says Job spoke rightly. Compare the lament psalms. How can believers bring raw grief to God?
- **Job and other ancient writings.** Belcher[^bto] compares Job with Egyptian and Babylonian works on suffering. How does Job's answer differ from their pessimism?

## Q&A

1. **Q:** How does God describe Job at the start?

   **A:** Blameless and upright, fearing God and turning away from evil (1:1, 8).

2. **Q:** What did Satan claim about Job?

   **A:** That Job feared God only because God had blessed him (1:9–11).

3. **Q:** What did Job say after losing everything?

   **A:** "The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord" (1:21).

4. **Q:** Who were Job's three friends?

   **A:** Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar (2:11).

5. **Q:** What did the friends believe about Job's suffering?

   **A:** That it must be punishment for his sin (4:7–8; 8:4–6; 22:4–5).

6. **Q:** What famous hope does Job express in chapter 19?

   **A:** "I know that my Redeemer lives", and that he would see God (19:25–27).

7. **Q:** Who speaks after the three friends fall silent?

   **A:** Elihu, a younger man (32:1–6).

8. **Q:** How did God answer Job?

   **A:** From the whirlwind, with questions about creation and his power (38–41).

9. **Q:** What did God say about the friends?

   **A:** They had not spoken rightly about him, as Job had (42:7).

10. **Q:** How did the book end for Job?

    **A:** He prayed for his friends, and God restored him with twice as much as before (42:10).
