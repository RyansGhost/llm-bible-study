# Habakkuk

## Summary

Habakkuk is a conversation between a troubled prophet and God. Habakkuk asks why God tolerates violence and injustice in Judah. God answers that he is raising up the Babylonians to judge them. That only deepens the problem: how can a holy God use a nation more wicked than Judah? God tells him to wait for the vision, because "the righteous shall live by his faith" (2:4). The book ends with Habakkuk rejoicing in God even if everything else fails.

## Setting

- **When:** most likely the reign of Jehoiakim (609–598 BC), when Judah slid back into wickedness after good King Josiah (Jer 22:13–19). Chapter 1 seems to come before 605 BC, while the Babylonians (Chaldeans) were rising but not yet dominant; chapters 2–3 after their victory at Carchemish that year (Reformation Study Bible). The Lutheran Study Bible gives about 605 BC, and the Reformation Heritage KJV Study Bible (RH) about 607/606 BC, just before Babylon's first invasion.
- **Where:** Judah and Jerusalem, near the temple (2:20; 3:19).
- **Who wrote it:** Habakkuk "the prophet" (1:1), perhaps well known in his day. Nothing else is known about him, and the meaning of his name is uncertain (RH suggests "embrace", fitting his clinging faith in 3:17–18). The musical note at the end (3:19) may link him to the temple singers. The legend in the apocryphal Bel and the Dragon that an angel carried him to Daniel in Babylon has no historical worth.
- **Where it sits:** among the Twelve, a contemporary of Jeremiah, Zephaniah and Nahum. Unusually, it isn't addressed to the people: it records the prophet's own struggle with God, in the pattern of lament psalms, and chapter 3 is a psalm with musical directions.

## Structure

The Reformation Study Bible's outline:

- 1:1: Heading
- 1:2–4: First complaint: why do the wicked in Judah prevail over the righteous?
- 1:5–11: First answer: the Lord will send the Babylonians
- 1:12–17: Second complaint: how can a holy God use the even more wicked Babylonians?
- 2: Second answer: wait for the vision; life for the faithful, and five woes on the proud
- 3: Habakkuk's prayer: a vision of God coming as at the exodus and Sinai, and a song of trust

## Chapter by chapter

- **Habakkuk 1:** "O Lord, how long shall I cry for help?" Violence and injustice fill Judah, and the law is paralysed. God answers that he is raising up the Chaldeans, fierce and swift. Habakkuk objects: you are too pure to look on evil, so why use a nation that sweeps up people like fish in a net?
- **Habakkuk 2:** Habakkuk waits on the watchtower. God says: write the vision plainly, for it will surely come. "The righteous shall live by his faith" (2:4). Five woes fall on Babylon for greed, plunder, violence, cruelty and idolatry. "The earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the Lord" (2:14). "The Lord is in his holy temple; let all the earth keep silence before him" (2:20).
- **Habakkuk 3:** A prayer-psalm. "In wrath remember mercy". Habakkuk recalls God marching out in power as at the exodus and Sinai. He trembles, but resolves: though the fig tree doesn't blossom and the fields yield no food, "yet I will rejoice in the Lord" (3:17–18).

## Key people

- **Habakkuk:** the prophet who questions God and learns to trust him.
- **The Chaldeans (Babylonians):** the fierce nation God raises up to judge Judah, and then judges in turn.

## Key events

- Habakkuk's complaints and God's answers (1–2)
- The vision of faith and the woes on Babylon (2)
- Habakkuk's prayer and resolve to rejoice (3)

## Themes

- **Wrestling honestly with God:** Habakkuk brings his questions about evil and injustice directly to God, then waits for his answer (2:1). He still holds on to God as his Rock and the Holy One (1:12–13).
- **When God seems silent:** Nahum shows God acting in judgment; Habakkuk faces the times when God's control isn't visible (RSB).
- **God's sovereignty over the nations:** God raises Babylon for judgment and will judge Babylon for its own sins (1:6; 2:6–17). Timmer shows how the Twelve treat both Israel's punishment through the nations and the punishment of the nations themselves.
- **Living by faith:** the righteous person trusts God and waits, even when God's ways are hard to understand (2:3–4). Paul builds on 2:4 for justification by faith (Rom 1:17; Gal 3:11; compare Heb 10:38), so the RSB calls Habakkuk a forefather of the Reformation.
- **The certainty of God's word:** the vision has an appointed time; it won't fail (2:3).
- **God's glory filling the earth:** history moves toward God's glory being known everywhere (2:14).
- **Prayer that submits to God's will:** complaint turns to a prayer that God would act as in the past (3:2), echoing "your will be done".
- **Joy in God alone:** faith that rejoices even when every earthly support is gone (3:17–19).

## How it points to Christ

"The righteous shall live by his faith" (2:4) is quoted three times in the New Testament. Paul uses it to show that we are justified by faith in Christ, not by works of the law (Rom 1:17; Gal 3:11), and Hebrews uses it to call believers to persevering faith while they wait for Christ's return (Heb 10:37–39). Martin Luther's rediscovery of this truth in Romans 1:17 helped spark the Reformation.

Habakkuk's prayer, "in wrath remember mercy" (3:2), is answered at the cross, where God's wrath against sin fell on Christ and mercy came to us.

## Common mix-ups

- Habakkuk complains twice, and God answers twice. Don't merge the two exchanges.
- The "Chaldeans" are the Babylonians.
- "The righteous shall live by his faith" comes from Habakkuk 2:4, though most people know it from Romans.
- "The Lord is in his holy temple; let all the earth keep silence" is Habakkuk 2:20, often used as a call to worship.
- Chapter 3 is a psalm ("according to Shigionoth"), with "Selah" markers like the Psalms.

## Going deeper

- **Is it right to question God?** Habakkuk asks "how long?" and "why?" God answers rather than rebukes him. How does this compare with Job and the lament psalms?
- **God using the wicked.** How can a holy God use Babylon to judge Judah, then judge Babylon (1:13; 2:6–17)? Compare Isaiah 10:5–15 on Assyria.
- **Living by faith (2:4).** What does "faith" mean for Habakkuk waiting for the vision, and how do Paul and Hebrews develop it?
- **Joy when everything fails (3:17–19).** What would that look like in your own life? What is the joy based on?
- **The day of the Lord and the nations.** Timmer shows how the Twelve look beyond immediate judgments to the final day. How does 2:14 point there?

## Q&A

1. **Q:** What was Habakkuk's first complaint?

   **A:** Why God allowed violence and injustice in Judah to go unpunished (1:2–4).

2. **Q:** How did God answer?

   **A:** He was raising up the Chaldeans (Babylonians) to bring judgment (1:5–6).

3. **Q:** What troubled Habakkuk about that answer?

   **A:** How a holy God could use a more wicked nation to punish Judah (1:13).

4. **Q:** What did God tell Habakkuk to do with the vision?

   **A:** Write it down plainly and wait for it (2:2–3).

5. **Q:** What does 2:4 say?

   **A:** "The righteous shall live by his faith."

6. **Q:** Where is 2:4 quoted in the New Testament?

   **A:** Romans 1:17, Galatians 3:11 and Hebrews 10:38.

7. **Q:** With what will the earth be filled, according to 2:14?

   **A:** The knowledge of the glory of the Lord.

8. **Q:** What did Habakkuk ask God to remember in wrath?

   **A:** Mercy (3:2).

9. **Q:** What will Habakkuk do even if the crops fail?

   **A:** Rejoice in the Lord and take joy in the God of his salvation (3:17–18).

10. **Q:** Who made Habakkuk 2:4 central to the Reformation?

    **A:** Martin Luther, through Romans 1:17.
