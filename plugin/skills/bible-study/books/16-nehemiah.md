# Nehemiah

## Summary

Nehemiah, a Jewish official in the Persian court, hears that Jerusalem's walls are still in ruins. After prayer and with the king's support, he goes to rebuild them, and despite mockery, threats and plots the wall is finished in fifty-two days. Then Ezra reads God's law to the people, they confess their sins and renew the covenant, and the wall is dedicated with great joy. But the book ends with Nehemiah confronting the same failures again, showing how much the people still needed a deeper renewal.

## Setting

- **When:** Nehemiah arrives in 445 BC, in Artaxerxes I's twentieth year. The last events happen after 433 BC, when Nehemiah returns for a second term (13:6) (Futato).
- **Where:** Susa, the Persian royal city, and Jerusalem with its broken walls and gates, surrounded by hostile neighbours: Samaria, Ammon, and the Arabs.
- **Who wrote it:** much of it is Nehemiah's own memoir, written in the first person (1–7; parts of 11–13). It was originally one book with Ezra. The Lutheran Study Bible names Nehemiah, governor of Judah, as author.
- **Where it sits:** the last historical events of the Old Testament. Malachi probably preaches around the same time, against the same sins.

## Structure

- 1–7: Rebuilding the walls
    - 1–2: Nehemiah's prayer, the king's permission, and his night inspection
    - 3: the builders and their sections
    - 4–6: opposition from outside and inside; the wall finished in 52 days
    - 7: guards set, and the list of returnees
- 8–10: Renewing the people: the law read, confession, and covenant renewal
- 11–13: Repopulating and dedicating the city, and Nehemiah's final reforms

## Chapter by chapter

- **Nehemiah 1:** In Susa, Nehemiah, the king's cupbearer, learns that Jerusalem's walls are broken down. He weeps, fasts, and prays a prayer of confession, asking for favour with the king.
- **Nehemiah 2:** King Artaxerxes grants his request and gives him letters and timber. Nehemiah inspects the walls by night and rallies the people. Sanballat, Tobiah and Geshem mock.
- **Nehemiah 3:** Families, priests, goldsmiths and perfumers each rebuild a section of the wall and its gates.
- **Nehemiah 4:** The enemies mock, then plot an attack. Nehemiah prays and sets guards; the builders work with a tool in one hand and a weapon in the other.
- **Nehemiah 5:** The poor cry out against wealthy Jews charging interest and enslaving their own people. Nehemiah rebukes them, and as governor refuses his own food allowance.
- **Nehemiah 6:** The enemies try tricks, slander and a false prophet to scare Nehemiah into hiding in the temple. He refuses, and the wall is finished in fifty-two days (6:15).
- **Nehemiah 7:** Gatekeepers and guards are appointed, and the list of the first returnees is found.
- **Nehemiah 8:** Ezra reads the Book of the Law from morning to midday, and the Levites help the people understand. The people weep, but are told, "the joy of the Lord is your strength" (8:10). They keep the Feast of Booths.
- **Nehemiah 9:** The people fast and confess. The Levites pray a great prayer reviewing God's faithfulness and Israel's rebellion from Abraham to the exile.
- **Nehemiah 10:** The leaders seal a written covenant: no marriage with pagans, keep the Sabbath, and support the temple with offerings and tithes.
- **Nehemiah 11:** One in ten of the people moves to Jerusalem to repopulate it.
- **Nehemiah 12:** The lists of priests and Levites. The wall is dedicated with two great choirs on top of it, and the joy is heard far away.
- **Nehemiah 13:** After time away, Nehemiah returns to find Tobiah living in a temple storeroom, the Levites unpaid, the Sabbath broken and mixed marriages again. He cleans house and prays, "Remember me, O my God, for good" (13:31).

## Key people

- **Nehemiah:** cupbearer to the Persian king, then governor of Judah. A man of prayer and decisive action.
- **Artaxerxes I:** king of Persia, who sends Nehemiah.
- **Sanballat the Horonite:** governor of Samaria and chief opponent.
- **Tobiah the Ammonite:** an opponent with connections inside Jerusalem.
- **Geshem the Arab:** a third opponent.
- **Ezra:** the priest and scribe who reads and teaches the law (8).
- **Eliashib:** the high priest, who lets Tobiah use a temple room (13:4–5).
- **Hanani:** Nehemiah's brother, who brings the news of Jerusalem (1:2).

## Themes

- **Prayer and action:** Nehemiah prays before everything: before the king (1:4–11; 2:4), under threat (4:9), and at the end (13:31). Then he works hard. Futato draws out God's sovereignty and human responsibility working together.
- **God's providence:** "the good hand of my God was upon me" (2:8, 18). God moves a Persian king and gives success against opponents.
- **The house of God rebuilt:** walls, temple and people restored together (Futato).
- **Ordinary people:** chapter 3 names families, guilds and even daughters who each rebuild their section.
- **The Word read and understood:** the Levites explain the law "so that the people understood the reading" (8:8). Futato highlights how important the written Word was for the years ahead, when no prophets would speak.
- **Justice in the community:** Nehemiah confronts the rich who exploit their poor brothers (5).
- **Repeated failure:** the book ends with the covenant of chapter 10 broken point by point (13), showing the need for changed hearts.

## How it points to Christ

Nehemiah left the comfort of the king's palace, identified with his people's shame, and gave himself to rebuild what was broken. That is a faint picture of Christ, who left heaven's glory to restore his people (Phil 2:5–8). Nehemiah's reforms couldn't hold: within years the people slid back (13). The book ends looking for a greater restorer who can change hearts, not just walls.

Jesus builds God's true house, his church, against which the gates of hell will not prevail (Matt 16:18). The new Jerusalem has walls and gates that will never fall or be shut (Rev 21:12–25).

## Common mix-ups

- Ezra rebuilds the temple and the people's worship; Nehemiah rebuilds Jerusalem's walls. The temple was finished about seventy years before Nehemiah arrived.
- Nehemiah was a cupbearer, a trusted royal official, not a priest or prophet.
- The wall took fifty-two days (6:15), not seven years.
- "The joy of the Lord is your strength" (8:10) was said to people weeping over their sins, not a general slogan.
- Sanballat, Tobiah and Geshem are different opponents from the ones in Ezra 4–5.
- Ezra and Nehemiah work together in Nehemiah 8 and 12. Their ministries overlap.

## Going deeper

- **Nehemiah's prayer (1:4–11).** He confesses, appeals to God's promises (Deut 30:1–5), and asks for specific help. How does it combine humility and boldness?
- **Leadership under pressure.** Nehemiah faces mockery, threats, internal injustice and personal plots (4–6). What does he do each time? How does prayer shape his responses?
- **Preaching the Word (8:1–8).** Reading plus explaining: many see this as a pattern for expository preaching. Read: Futato, The Written Word.
- **The great prayer (9).** A sweeping review of salvation history. What does it say about God's patience and Israel's repeated rebellion? Compare Psalms 78, 105 and 106.
- **Why does the book end in failure?** Nehemiah 13 reverses chapter 10 almost item by item. What does this teach about reform, willpower and the need for the new covenant (Jer 31:31–34)?

## Q&A

1. What was Nehemiah's job in Persia? Cupbearer to King Artaxerxes (1:11).
2. What news made Nehemiah weep? That Jerusalem's wall was broken down and its gates burned (1:3–4).
3. What did Nehemiah do before answering the king? He prayed silently to God (2:4–5).
4. Who were Nehemiah's main opponents? Sanballat, Tobiah and Geshem (2:19).
5. How did the builders protect themselves? Working with a tool in one hand and a weapon in the other, with guards posted (4:16–18).
6. How long did the wall take to finish? Fifty-two days (6:15).
7. Who read the law to the people? Ezra (8:1–3).
8. What did Nehemiah tell the weeping people? "Do not be grieved, for the joy of the Lord is your strength" (8:10).
9. What did the people promise in the covenant of chapter 10? Not to marry pagans, to keep the Sabbath, and to support the temple.
10. What problems did Nehemiah find on his return? Tobiah in the temple, unpaid Levites, Sabbath-breaking and mixed marriages (13).
