# Revelation

## Summary

Revelation is "the revelation of Jesus Christ" (1:1), given to John in a series of visions while he was exiled on the island of Patmos. Hill sums it up as showing the paradoxical present rule of Jesus Christ as King over all the kings of the earth, his final triumph, and the salvation of his people through tribulation. The risen Christ speaks to seven churches in Asia. Then John sees God's throne in heaven, where the Lamb who was slain takes the scroll of history. Seals, trumpets and bowls of judgment follow. The dragon and the beasts make war on the saints, and Babylon the great seduces the nations. But Babylon falls, Christ returns as the rider on the white horse, Satan is defeated, the dead are judged, and God makes all things new. The book ends in a new heaven and new earth, where God dwells with his people and wipes away every tear. Its message for every age: be faithful, even to death, because the Lamb has conquered.

## Setting

- **When:** most likely about AD 95, near the end of the emperor Domitian's reign (AD 81–96), as Irenaeus reports, and as most scholars hold (Reformation Study Bible). Some persecution had already fallen (1:9; 2:13), more was coming (2:10), and false teaching and fading zeal tempted the churches to compromise. Hill gives reasons from the book itself for a date after Jerusalem fell in AD 70. Some, especially preterists, date it before AD 70, under Nero.
- **Where:** John received the visions on Patmos, a small island off the coast of Asia Minor, where he was exiled "on account of the word of God" (1:9). He wrote to seven churches in the Roman province of Asia (1:11).
- **Who wrote it:** John (1:1, 4, 9; 22:8). Hill argues this is John the apostle, son of Zebedee: only he had the stature to address seven churches by his bare name. Justin Martyr, Irenaeus and many early writers agree. Dionysius of Alexandria later doubted it because the Greek style differs from John's Gospel, but Hill notes the very different kind of writing explains this.
- **Where it sits:** the last book of the Bible, and the one prophetic book of the New Testament. Hill describes it as an apocalyptic prophecy in the form of a letter, steeped in imagery from Daniel, Ezekiel, Isaiah, Zechariah, the Psalms and Genesis. It alludes to the Old Testament more than the rest of the New Testament put together. God "made it known" by signs (1:1), the word the Greek Old Testament uses for Nebuchadnezzar's symbolic dream (Dan 2:45), so the RSB says to read it as symbolic unless the text demands otherwise. Like Jesus' parables, the symbols reveal truth to those with ears to hear (2:7).

## Structure

The Reformation Study Bible's outline is simple: introduction (1), letters to the seven churches (2–3), heavenly visions (4:1–22:5), final exhortations (22:6–20) and closing blessing (22:21). It reads the visions as seven cycles that run in parallel, each viewing the time up to Christ's return from a different angle: 4:1–8:1; 8:2–11:19; 12–14; 15–16; 17:1–19:10; 19:11–21; 20. The characters also enter in one order and are judged in reverse: God's people, the dragon, the beast and false prophet, the bride, and Babylon; then Babylon falls, the bride is married, the beast and false prophet are destroyed, then the dragon, and God's people shine in the new creation. Hill structures the book around four main visions, each beginning with John "in the Spirit" (1:10; 4:2; 17:3; 21:10):

- 1:1–8: Prologue
- 1:9–3:22: First vision: the Son of Man among the lampstands, and his letters to the seven churches
- 4:1–16:21: Second vision: the Lamb reigns from the heavenly throne room
    - 4–5: worship of the Creator and the Redeemer; the Lamb takes the scroll
    - 6:1–8:5: the seven seals (with the 144,000 and the great multitude, 7)
    - 8:6–11:19: the seven trumpets (with the little scroll and the two witnesses, 10–11)
    - 12–14: the dragon, the woman, the two beasts, and the Lamb on Mount Zion
    - 15–16: the seven bowls
- 17:1–21:8: Third vision: the judgment of Babylon, the beasts, Satan, and death
- 21:9–22:5: Fourth vision: the new Jerusalem
- 22:6–21: Epilogue

## Chapter by chapter

- **Revelation 1:** The revelation of Jesus Christ, with a blessing on those who read and keep it. Grace from the one who is and was and is to come, the seven spirits, and Jesus Christ, who loves us and freed us from our sins by his blood. On Patmos, on the Lord's day, John sees one like a son of man among seven golden lampstands, with eyes like fire and a voice like many waters. "I am the first and the last, and the living one. I died, and behold I am alive forevermore" (1:17–18).
- **Revelation 2:** Letters to Ephesus (you have abandoned your first love), Smyrna (be faithful unto death, and I will give you the crown of life), Pergamum (some hold the teaching of Balaam), and Thyatira (you tolerate Jezebel).
- **Revelation 3:** Letters to Sardis (you have a name for being alive, but are dead), Philadelphia (I have set before you an open door), and Laodicea (you are lukewarm; "Behold, I stand at the door and knock", 3:20). To each: whoever conquers will be rewarded.
- **Revelation 4:** A door opens in heaven. John sees God on his throne, surrounded by a rainbow, twenty-four elders and four living creatures who never stop saying, "Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord God Almighty". The elders cast their crowns before him, the Creator.
- **Revelation 5:** No one is worthy to open the sealed scroll. Then the Lion of the tribe of Judah has conquered: John looks and sees a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain. He takes the scroll, and heaven sings a new song: "Worthy are you … for you were slain, and by your blood you ransomed people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation" (5:9).
- **Revelation 6:** The Lamb opens six seals: the four horsemen (conquest, war, famine, death), the martyrs under the altar crying "How long?", and a great earthquake as people hide from the wrath of the Lamb.
- **Revelation 7:** Before judgment falls, 144,000 from the tribes of Israel are sealed. Then John sees a great multitude no one could number, from every nation, clothed in white, who have come out of the great tribulation and washed their robes in the blood of the Lamb. God will wipe away every tear from their eyes.
- **Revelation 8:** The seventh seal brings silence in heaven. The prayers of the saints rise with incense. Seven angels blow trumpets: hail, fire and blood strike a third of the earth, sea, rivers and heavens.
- **Revelation 9:** The fifth and sixth trumpets: locusts from the bottomless pit torment those without God's seal, and a vast cavalry kills a third of mankind. The rest still don't repent of their idols and immorality.
- **Revelation 10:** A mighty angel with a little scroll declares there will be no more delay. John eats the scroll: sweet in his mouth, bitter in his stomach. He must prophesy again.
- **Revelation 11:** John measures the temple. The two witnesses prophesy for 1,260 days, are killed by the beast, then raised and taken up to heaven. The seventh trumpet: "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ, and he shall reign forever and ever" (11:15).
- **Revelation 12:** A woman clothed with the sun gives birth to a male child who will rule the nations. A great red dragon seeks to devour him, but the child is caught up to God. Michael and his angels throw the dragon, Satan, down from heaven. The saints conquer him "by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony" (12:11). The dragon makes war on the woman's offspring.
- **Revelation 13:** A beast rises from the sea, given authority by the dragon, blaspheming God and making war on the saints. A second beast from the earth deceives people into worshipping the first and forces everyone to take its mark. Its number is 666.
- **Revelation 14:** The Lamb stands on Mount Zion with the 144,000. Angels proclaim the eternal gospel, the fall of Babylon, and judgment on those who worship the beast. "Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord" (14:13). The earth is reaped in two harvests.
- **Revelation 15:** Those who conquered the beast sing the song of Moses and of the Lamb. Seven angels receive seven bowls full of God's wrath.
- **Revelation 16:** The bowls are poured out: sores, the sea and rivers turned to blood, scorching sun, darkness, the Euphrates dried up for the battle at Armageddon. The seventh bowl: "It is done!" Babylon is shaken and falls apart.
- **Revelation 17:** John sees the great prostitute, Babylon, sitting on a scarlet beast with seven heads and ten horns, drunk with the blood of the saints. The beast and the kings will make war on the Lamb, and the Lamb will conquer them, for he is Lord of lords and King of kings.
- **Revelation 18:** "Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great!" Come out of her, my people. Kings, merchants and sailors mourn her sudden ruin.
- **Revelation 19:** Heaven rejoices: "Hallelujah! For the Lord our God the Almighty reigns". The marriage supper of the Lamb is announced. Heaven opens and the rider on a white horse, Faithful and True, the Word of God, comes to judge and make war. The beast and the false prophet are thrown into the lake of fire.
- **Revelation 20:** Satan is bound for a thousand years, and the saints reign with Christ. Then Satan is released, gathers the nations against the saints, and is thrown into the lake of fire forever. The dead, great and small, are judged before a great white throne. Death and Hades are thrown into the lake of fire. Anyone not found in the book of life is thrown in too.
- **Revelation 21:** A new heaven and a new earth. The holy city, new Jerusalem, comes down from God as a bride. "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man". He will wipe away every tear, and death shall be no more. "Behold, I am making all things new" (21:5). The city shines with God's glory: it has no temple, for its temple is the Lord God and the Lamb.
- **Revelation 22:** The river of the water of life flows from the throne, and the tree of life yields fruit and leaves for the healing of the nations. They will see his face. "Behold, I am coming soon". Don't add to or take away from the words of this book. "Come, Lord Jesus!" (22:20).

## Key people

- **Jesus Christ:** the Son of Man, the Lion of Judah, the Lamb who was slain, the rider on the white horse, the Alpha and Omega.
- **God the Father:** seated on the throne, the Creator and Almighty.
- **John:** the apostle, exiled on Patmos, who sees and records the visions.
- **The seven churches:** Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia and Laodicea.
- **The twenty-four elders and four living creatures:** worshippers around the throne (4–5).
- **The two witnesses:** who prophesy, are killed and are raised (11).
- **The woman and her child:** God's people, from whom the Messiah comes (12).
- **Michael:** the archangel who casts the dragon out of heaven (12:7).
- **The dragon (Satan), the beast and the false prophet:** the unholy alliance opposing God (12–13; 19–20).
- **Babylon the great prostitute:** the seductive world city opposed to God (17–18).
- **The bride of the Lamb:** the church, the new Jerusalem (19:7; 21:9).

## Key events

- John's vision of the risen Christ on Patmos (1)
- The Lamb taking the scroll in the heavenly throne room (5)
- The seals, trumpets and bowls of judgment (6–16)
- The war in heaven and Satan's defeat (12)
- The fall of Babylon (18)
- The return of Christ and the defeat of the beast (19)
- The binding of Satan, the thousand years and the final judgment (20)
- The new heaven, new earth and new Jerusalem (21–22)

## Themes

- **God on his throne:** "throne" appears 34 times, half of them in chapters 4–5. God rules history and will bring it to its goal in Christ, so his people worship him (RSB).
- **Christ reigns now:** Hill stresses the Lamb's present rule from the throne, though his people suffer (5; 12:10). The last days began with his first coming and will be completed at his return (1:5–6).
- **Victory through the cross:** the Lamb conquers by being slain, and his people conquer by his blood and their faithful witness, even to death (5:5–6; 12:11).
- **Worship:** heaven's songs to God the Creator and the Lamb the Redeemer run through the book (4–5; 7; 15; 19).
- **Faithful witness under pressure:** Christ's letters call the churches to repent, hold fast and conquer (2–3).
- **God's righteous judgment:** on the persecutors and the unrepentant, answering the martyrs' "How long?" (6:10; 16:5–7; 19:1–2).
- **The church against the world system:** Babylon's seduction of wealth and idolatry, and the call to "come out of her" (18:4).
- **Satan's counterfeits:** the dragon, the beast and the false prophet form an unholy imitation of the Trinity (16:13): the beast mimics Christ, even with a death-wound healed (13:3), and the false prophet mimics the Spirit by directing worship to the beast. Babylon the prostitute counterfeits the Lamb's bride (17; 19:7–8) (RSB).
- **The final defeat of evil:** Satan, the beasts, death and Hades are thrown into the lake of fire (19–20).
- **New creation:** God dwelling with his people in a renewed creation, Eden restored and surpassed (21–22).

## How it points to Christ

The book's title is its theme: "the revelation of Jesus Christ" (1:1). He is "the faithful witness, the firstborn of the dead, and the ruler of kings on earth" (1:5), the Son of Man of Daniel 7, the first and the last. The book's central image is the Lion of Judah who turns out to be a Lamb that was slain (5:5–6): he conquers by his sacrifice. He holds the scroll of history and opens its seals. Heaven worships him alongside the one on the throne (5:13). He returns as Faithful and True, King of kings and Lord of lords (19:11–16). In the new Jerusalem, God and the Lamb are its temple and its light (21:22–23). He is "the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end" (22:13), and the Bible ends with the prayer, "Come, Lord Jesus!"

## Common mix-ups

- The book is "Revelation", singular, not "Revelations".
- Hill describes four main approaches. Historicist: the book traces church history in order (the view of almost all the Reformers, who saw the papacy as antichrist). Preterist: it was fulfilled mainly in the first century. Futurist: most of it concerns the final years before Christ returns (popular today through dispensationalism). Idealist: it shows principles true in every age. Hill thinks the idealist approach is most useful, grounded in Christ and allowing for a final future fulfilment; the RSB thinks a combination of the views is closest to the truth, since its images can be fulfilled more than once.
- The thousand years (20:1–6). Amillennialists, including Hill and many Reformed churches, read it as Christ's present reign with his saints between his first and second comings. Premillennialists expect a literal thousand-year reign on earth after Christ returns. Postmillennialists expect a long golden age of gospel success before he returns. The Reformed confessions describe one return of Christ followed by the general resurrection and judgment (WCF 32–33).
- Revelation isn't a strict timeline. Hill points to "recapitulation": the book repeatedly brings us to the edge of the end (6:12–17; 11:15–19; 14:14–20; 16:17–21; 19:11–21; 20:11–15), then goes back to show the same period from another angle.
- The word "antichrist" doesn't appear in Revelation. It is in 1 and 2 John.
- "Behold, I stand at the door and knock" (3:20) is spoken to a lukewarm church, calling it to repent and restore fellowship. It is often used in evangelism, but that isn't its first meaning.
- Revelation 22:18–19 warns against adding to or taking away from "this book". Many also apply it to the closed canon of Scripture as a whole.
- Luther first doubted Revelation's place in 1522, but his 1530 preface treated it more favourably, reading it largely as church history (Lutheran Study Bible).

## Going deeper

- **How should we read Revelation?** Compare the four approaches Hill describes. Which do the Reformers, modern dispensationalists and Hill favour, and why?
- **The Lion and the Lamb (5).** What does it mean that the conquering Lion turns out to be a slain Lamb? How does that reshape our idea of power and victory?
- **The seven churches (2–3).** Which church most resembles yours? What does Christ commend and correct?
- **Recapitulation.** Do the seals, trumpets and bowls follow one another in time, or show the same period from different angles? What difference does it make?
- **The millennium (20).** Set out the amillennial, premillennial and postmillennial views. What does Hill argue about the "first resurrection"? How do the confessions (WCF 32–33) frame the end?
- **Babylon (17–18).** Who or what is Babylon? How does Revelation call Christians to resist the idols of wealth, power and comfort?
- **All things new (21–22).** How does the end of the Bible echo Genesis 1–3? What will be restored, and what will be better than Eden?

## Q&A

1. **Q:** What is the book called in its first verse?

   **A:** The revelation of Jesus Christ (1:1).

2. **Q:** Where was John when he received the visions?

   **A:** On the island of Patmos (1:9).

3. **Q:** To how many churches does Christ send letters?

   **A:** Seven, in the province of Asia (1:11; 2–3).

4. **Q:** Which church had abandoned its first love?

   **A:** Ephesus (2:4).

5. **Q:** Which church was lukewarm?

   **A:** Laodicea (3:15–16).

6. **Q:** Who alone was worthy to open the scroll?

   **A:** The Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Lamb who was slain (5:5–9).

7. **Q:** What three series of seven judgments are there?

   **A:** Seals, trumpets and bowls (6:1–8:1; 8:6–11:19; 16:1–21).

8. **Q:** How did the saints conquer the dragon?

   **A:** By the blood of the Lamb and the word of their testimony (12:11).

9. **Q:** What happens to Satan at the end of the thousand years?

   **A:** He is released, then defeated and thrown into the lake of fire forever (20:7–10).

10. **Q:** What does God say from the throne in 21:5?

    **A:** "Behold, I am making all things new."
