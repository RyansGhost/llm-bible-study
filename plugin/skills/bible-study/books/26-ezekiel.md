# Ezekiel

## Summary

Ezekiel was a priest carried into exile in Babylon. There God showed him visions of his glory and sent him to warn a stubborn people. He saw God's glory leave the defiled temple before Jerusalem's destruction, and pronounced judgment on Judah and the nations. After the city fell, his message turned to hope: God will shepherd his people himself, give them new hearts and his Spirit, raise the nation like dry bones, and return in glory to a new temple where "the Lord is there".

## Setting

- **When:** Ezekiel was taken to Babylon with King Jehoiachin in 597 BC, at about 26. He was called in 593 BC, aged thirty, the age a priest would have begun serving at the temple, and his last dated oracle is from 571 BC (29:17). The book dates everything by the years of Jehoiachin's exile, treating him as the last rightful king of David's line (Reformation Study Bible). The fall of Jerusalem (586 BC) is the hinge of the book (33:21).
- **Where:** among the exiles by the Chebar canal in Babylonia, probably at Tel-abib (1:1–3; 3:15). In visions he is taken to Jerusalem (8–11; 40–48). The exiles were tempted to despair, feeling cut off from God and surrounded by Babylon's apparently triumphant gods (33:10; 37:11).
- **Who wrote it:** Ezekiel son of Buzi, a priest (1:3), whose name means "God makes strong". He writes mostly in the first person, with precise dates, probably completing the book soon after 571 BC. McKelvey notes that a few 20th-century scholars questioned its unity, but most accept it as substantially Ezekiel's; its unified structure and priestly outlook point to one author (RSB).
- **Where it sits:** the third Major Prophet, a younger contemporary of Jeremiah (in Jerusalem) and Daniel (in Babylon's court). Revelation draws heavily on it.

## Structure

Three movements, also found in Isaiah and Zephaniah: judgment on God's people, judgment on the nations, then mercy for God's people. The news of Jerusalem's fall frames the oracles against the nations (24:26–27; 33:21–22) and turns the book from judgment to hope (McKelvey; Reformation Study Bible):

- 1–3: Ezekiel's call and commission: the vision of God's glory, the scroll, the watchman
- 4–24: Judgment on Judah and Jerusalem: sign-acts, the glory departing, parables and indictments
- 25–32: Oracles against seven nations: Ammon, Moab, Edom, Philistia, Tyre, Sidon and Egypt
- 33–48: Blessing for Judah and Jerusalem: the watchman renewed, the true Shepherd, new hearts, dry bones, Gog, and the new temple and land

## Chapter by chapter

- **Ezekiel 1:** By the Chebar canal (593 BC), Ezekiel sees a storm, four living creatures, wheels full of eyes, and above them a throne with the appearance of the glory of the Lord.
- **Ezekiel 2:** God calls him "son of man" and sends him to a rebellious house, whether they listen or not, with a scroll of lament and woe.
- **Ezekiel 3:** He eats the scroll and it tastes sweet as honey. God makes him a watchman for Israel (3:17), responsible to warn them, and he is struck mute except when God gives him words.
- **Ezekiel 4:** He acts out Jerusalem's siege with a brick, lies on his side for many days, and eats rationed bread.
- **Ezekiel 5:** He shaves his head and divides the hair into thirds, burned, struck and scattered, picturing the city's fate. A few hairs are kept.
- **Ezekiel 6:** Judgment against the mountains of Israel and their idol shrines.
- **Ezekiel 7:** "An end has come". The day of disaster is near.
- **Ezekiel 8:** In a vision he is taken to Jerusalem and sees idolatry in the temple itself: images, women weeping for Tammuz, men worshipping the sun.
- **Ezekiel 9:** Executioners strike the city. A mark is first placed on those who grieve over its abominations.
- **Ezekiel 10:** The glory of the Lord rises from the temple to the threshold, carried by the cherubim.
- **Ezekiel 11:** Judgment on the leaders, and a promise: God will give them one heart and a new spirit (11:19). The glory leaves the city for the mountain to the east.
- **Ezekiel 12:** He acts out going into exile, digging through the wall with his baggage. The prince (Zedekiah) won't see Babylon.
- **Ezekiel 13:** False prophets whitewash a crumbling wall and say "peace".
- **Ezekiel 14:** Elders have idols in their hearts. Even Noah, Daniel and Job could save only themselves.
- **Ezekiel 15:** Jerusalem is a useless vine branch, fit only for burning.
- **Ezekiel 16:** Jerusalem was an abandoned baby God rescued and married, but she became a faithless wife. Yet God will remember his covenant.
- **Ezekiel 17:** A riddle of two eagles and a vine; God will plant a tender sprig of cedar on a high mountain.
- **Ezekiel 18:** Each person is responsible for his own sin: "the soul who sins shall die". God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked. "Turn, and live" (18:32).
- **Ezekiel 19:** A lament for Israel's princes, pictured as captured lions and an uprooted vine.
- **Ezekiel 20:** Israel's history of rebellion; God has acted for the sake of his name.
- **Ezekiel 21:** The sword of the Lord is drawn. Babylon's king consults omens at a fork in the road.
- **Ezekiel 22:** Jerusalem's sins listed. God looked for someone to stand in the breach, and found none (22:30).
- **Ezekiel 23:** Two adulterous sisters, Oholah (Samaria) and Oholibah (Jerusalem).
- **Ezekiel 24:** A boiling pot pictures the siege, which begins that day. Ezekiel's wife dies, and he is forbidden to mourn openly, as a sign.
- **Ezekiel 25:** Oracles against Ammon, Moab, Edom and Philistia.
- **Ezekiel 26:** Tyre will be destroyed and scraped bare like a rock.
- **Ezekiel 27:** A lament for Tyre, pictured as a great merchant ship that sinks.
- **Ezekiel 28:** The proud king of Tyre, pictured as once in Eden, is cast down. Sidon is judged, and Israel will be regathered.
- **Ezekiel 29:** Egypt, the great monster of the Nile, judged.
- **Ezekiel 30:** The day of the Lord against Egypt.
- **Ezekiel 31:** Pharaoh as a great cedar that falls, as Assyria did.
- **Ezekiel 32:** A lament over Pharaoh going down to the pit with the fallen nations.
- **Ezekiel 33:** The watchman's duty renewed. News arrives that Jerusalem has fallen, and Ezekiel's mouth is opened.
- **Ezekiel 34:** Woe to the shepherds who fed themselves. God himself will seek and shepherd his sheep, and set over them one shepherd, "my servant David".
- **Ezekiel 35:** Judgment on Mount Seir (Edom) for its hatred of Israel.
- **Ezekiel 36:** The mountains of Israel will flourish again. God will sprinkle clean water, give a new heart and spirit, and put his Spirit within them (36:25–27), for his name's sake.
- **Ezekiel 37:** The valley of dry bones comes to life as God's breath enters them. Two sticks become one: one people under one king, with an everlasting covenant of peace.
- **Ezekiel 38:** Gog of the land of Magog leads nations against restored Israel.
- **Ezekiel 39:** Gog is utterly defeated, and God pours out his Spirit on Israel.
- **Ezekiel 40:** In 573 BC Ezekiel is shown a vision of a new temple, and a man measures its gates and courts.
- **Ezekiel 41:** The temple's inner rooms are measured.
- **Ezekiel 42:** The priests' chambers and the outer wall, separating holy from common.
- **Ezekiel 43:** The glory of the Lord returns from the east and fills the temple. The altar is described.
- **Ezekiel 44:** The east gate stays shut. Rules for Levites and the faithful Zadokite priests.
- **Ezekiel 45:** The holy district, the prince's land, and just measures and offerings.
- **Ezekiel 46:** Regulations for worship and for the prince.
- **Ezekiel 47:** A river flows from the temple, growing deeper, bringing life wherever it goes and healing the Dead Sea. The land's boundaries.
- **Ezekiel 48:** The land divided among the tribes. The city's name: "The Lord is there" (48:35).

## Key people

- **Ezekiel:** a priest in exile, called "son of man" (a mortal, frail before God, unlike the messianic title in Daniel 7:13), and a watchman to Israel.
- **Ezekiel's wife:** her death becomes a sign of Jerusalem's fall (24:15–27).
- **The elders of Israel:** exile leaders who come to Ezekiel with idols in their hearts (8; 14; 20).
- **Jehoiachin:** the exiled king by whose captivity the book is dated.
- **Zedekiah:** the prince left in Jerusalem, who will be taken to Babylon blinded (12:12–13).
- **Gog of Magog:** the leader of the final attack on God's people (38–39).
- **"My servant David":** the coming shepherd-king (34:23–24; 37:24–25).

## Key events

- The vision of God's glory by the Chebar canal (1)
- The call, and eating the scroll (2–3)
- Sign-acts of the siege (4–5)
- The vision of idolatry in the temple (8)
- The glory leaves the temple and the city (10–11)
- Ezekiel's wife dies; the siege begins (24)
- News of Jerusalem's fall (33:21)
- The promise of the good Shepherd (34)
- New heart and Spirit (36)
- The valley of dry bones (37)
- The battle against Gog (38–39)
- The new temple, and the glory's return (40–48)

## Themes

- **The glory of God:** McKelvey highlights the temple and God's glory as a key to the book. The glory departs because of sin (10–11) and returns to a new temple (43), a pattern he describes as creation, de-creation and re-creation.
- **God's holiness:** the root for "holy" appears about 51 times. God's holy name has been profaned by his people and must be vindicated (36:22–23).
- **God's sovereignty:** "they will know that I am the Lord" recurs throughout. God rules Israel and the nations to make himself known.
- **Sin and responsibility:** no prophet catalogues Israel's history of sin more fully (16; 20; 23). Each generation, and each person, answers for its own sin, not its fathers' (18).
- **Sign-acts and visions:** Ezekiel acts out his message (lying on his side, shaving his head, packing an exile's bag) because his hearers resisted plain words (4–5; 12).
- **The watchman:** the prophet must warn the wicked, and is accountable if he stays silent (3; 33).
- **God's mercy and the remnant:** even in judgment God keeps a remnant, and has no pleasure in the death of the wicked (5:3; 18:23).
- **New heart and Spirit:** the deepest hope is inner renewal by God's Spirit, something God does for his people because they can't do it themselves (11:19–20; 36:26–27; 37:14).
- **A restoration still to come:** the new temple still restricts access and needs sacrifices (40–46). Its fullness comes in Christ and the new Jerusalem, where God's presence fills the whole city (Rev 21–22) (RSB).

## How it points to Christ

Jesus is the good Shepherd God promised: God himself coming to seek his scattered sheep, and the true "David" who shepherds them (34; John 10:11–16). The promise of cleansing water, a new heart and the Spirit (36:25–27) lies behind Jesus' words about being born of water and the Spirit (John 3:5). The dry bones brought to life by God's breath picture the new life the Spirit gives, and the resurrection to come.

The glory that left the temple returns in Jesus, the Word who "dwelt among us" and showed God's glory (John 1:14). McKelvey notes how much John draws on Ezekiel. The river of life flowing from the temple (47) becomes the living water Jesus gives (John 7:37–39) and the river of the water of life in the new Jerusalem (Rev 22:1–2). The city named "The Lord is there" is fulfilled when "the dwelling place of God is with man" (Rev 21:3).

## Common mix-ups

- Ezekiel lived in Babylon, not Jerusalem. He saw Jerusalem only in visions until it fell.
- "Son of man" for Ezekiel means "mortal human", stressing his weakness before God. Jesus' use of the title draws more on Daniel 7.
- The valley of dry bones (37) is first about the restoration of Israel as a nation, which also anticipates resurrection.
- The Daniel mentioned with Noah and Job (14:14) may be the famous exile Daniel, or an ancient righteous man of the same name. Scholars debate it.
- Gog and Magog appear here (38–39) and again in Revelation 20:8.
- Ezekiel 28's lament over the king of Tyre is often applied to Satan's fall, but it is first addressed to a human king.
- Ezekiel was silent between his call and the news of Jerusalem's fall, except when God gave him a message (3:26–27; 33:22).

## Going deeper

- **The departure and return of the glory.** Trace God's glory from chapter 1 through 8–11 to 43. Why must the glory leave before it can return? How does this frame the whole book? Read: McKelvey, Creation, De-creation, and Re-creation.
- **Individual responsibility (18).** Does Ezekiel 18 contradict the idea that children suffer for their parents' sin (Ex 20:5)? What is the point for the exiles who blamed their ancestors?
- **The new heart (36:25–27).** Compare with Jeremiah's new covenant (Jer 31:31–34). How do Reformed theologians connect this with regeneration?
- **Ezekiel's sign-acts.** Lying on his side, shaving his head, digging through a wall, not mourning his wife: why does God use such dramatic actions? What do they cost the prophet?
- **How should the temple vision (40–48) be read?** A literal future temple, a symbolic picture of God dwelling with his people fulfilled in Christ and the church, or the new creation of Revelation 21–22? Read McKelvey's discussion of John's use of Ezekiel.
- **"They will know that I am the Lord."** Count how often it appears. What does it say about God's purpose in both judgment and salvation?

## Q&A

1. **Q:** Where was Ezekiel when he received his first vision?

   **A:** By the Chebar canal in Babylon, among the exiles (1:1–3).

2. **Q:** What did Ezekiel see in his first vision?

   **A:** Four living creatures, wheels, and the throne and glory of God (1:4–28).

3. **Q:** What did God tell Ezekiel to eat?

   **A:** A scroll, which tasted as sweet as honey (3:1–3).

4. **Q:** What role did God give Ezekiel?

   **A:** A watchman for the house of Israel (3:17; 33:7).

5. **Q:** What did Ezekiel see in the temple in chapter 8?

   **A:** Idolatry: images, weeping for Tammuz, and sun worship (8:5–16).

6. **Q:** What happened to the glory of the Lord in chapters 10–11?

   **A:** It left the temple and the city (10:18–19; 11:22–23).

7. **Q:** What sign did God give when Ezekiel's wife died?

   **A:** He was not to mourn openly, as a sign of Jerusalem's fall (24:15–27).

8. **Q:** Who did God say would shepherd his people?

   **A:** God himself, through "my servant David" (34:11–15, 23–24).

9. **Q:** What did God promise in Ezekiel 36:26?

   **A:** A new heart and a new spirit: a heart of flesh in place of a heart of stone.

10. **Q:** What is the city called at the end of the book?

    **A:** "The Lord is there" (48:35).
