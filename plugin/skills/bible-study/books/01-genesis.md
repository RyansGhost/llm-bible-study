# Genesis

## Summary

Genesis ("origin"; in Hebrew *bereshit*, "in the beginning") is the book of beginnings: of the world, of humanity, of sin, and of God's plan to save. God makes humans to rule the earth under him, but they fall into sin, and God at once promises that the woman's "offspring" will defeat the serpent (3:15). He carries that promise through a chosen family line, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph, by covenant and often by choosing the younger over the older, keeping it alive through every family failure. Genesis opens the long story that runs through to Kings, and sets the scene for the whole Bible.

## Setting

- **When:** from creation to Joseph's death in Egypt. The events before Abraham can't be dated. The patriarchs were a semi-nomadic family living around 2000 BC, and names and customs in chapters 12–50 fit that era (*RSB*[^rsb]). Moses probably wrote in the fifteenth century BC (*RSB*[^rsb]; the *Lutheran Study Bible*[^lsb] gives about 1446–1406 BC).
- **Where:** Eden, Mesopotamia (Ur, Haran, Babel), Canaan (Shechem, Bethel, Hebron, Beersheba), and finally Egypt.
- **Who wrote it:** Moses gave Genesis its substance, probably using older oral and written sources. Later inspired editors updated some place names (14:14) and added a few notes, such as the list of Edom's kings "before any king reigned over Israel" (36:31). Jesus treats the Pentateuch as Moses' (John 7:22). The *Reformation Heritage KJV Study Bible* (*RH*[^rh]) places the writing in the wilderness years, so that a people fresh from Egypt's many gods would learn who their God is and where they came from. The *RSB*[^rsb] and Currid[^bto] both reject the modern theory that Genesis was stitched together from conflicting documents (J, E, D and P).
- **Where it sits:** the first of the five books of Moses (the Pentateuch), and the start of one continuous history running from creation to the fall of Jerusalem (Genesis–Kings). Chapters 1–11 echo, and deliberately contradict, older Near Eastern creation and flood stories such as *Enuma Elish* and *Atrahasis*.

## Structure

Currid[^bto] divides Genesis into three sections: creation, the early history of the world, and the patriarchs. Within them, a recurring heading, "These are the generations (*toledoth*) of …", opens each new family story; Currid[^bto] counts eleven, and the *RSB*[^rsb] treats the two for Esau (36:1, 9) as one account, giving ten. The last four set rejected lines (Ishmael, Esau) beside chosen ones (Isaac, Jacob). The nested sections follow the *RSB*[^rsb]:

- 1:1–2:3: Prologue: creation of the heavens and the earth
- 2:4–11:26: The early history of the world, from Adam to Abraham
    - 2:4–4:26: the heavens and the earth: Eden, the fall, Cain and Abel
    - 5:1–6:8: Adam: the godly line of Seth; sin before the flood
    - 6:9–9:29: Noah: the flood and God's covenant
    - 10:1–11:9: Noah's sons: the table of nations and Babel
    - 11:10–26: Shem
- 11:27–50:26: The patriarchs
    - 11:27–25:11: Terah: Abraham and God's covenant promises
    - 25:12–18: Ishmael
    - 25:19–35:29: Isaac: Jacob and Esau
    - 36:1–37:1: Esau
    - 37:2–50:26: Jacob: Joseph, Judah, and the move to Egypt

Currid[^bto] also reads Genesis 1–3 as the whole Bible's message in miniature: creation, fall and redemption. *RH*[^rh] divides the book more simply into three parts: the world made and ruined (1–11), God's covenant with the three patriarchs (12–36), and the crisis over Joseph and Judah (37–50).

## Chapter by chapter

- **Genesis 1:** God creates the heavens and the earth in six days by his word, and everything is "very good". Man and woman are made in God's image to rule the earth.
- **Genesis 2:** God rests on the seventh day. He forms the man from dust, places him in Eden with one command about the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, and makes the woman from his side. Marriage begins.
- **Genesis 3:** The serpent deceives the woman; she and the man eat and hide in shame. God curses the serpent and promises that the woman's offspring will crush its head (3:15). Adam and Eve are sent out of the garden.
- **Genesis 4:** Cain murders Abel and is driven out. Cain's line builds cities and grows violent (Lamech). Seth is born, and people begin to call on the name of the Lord.
- **Genesis 5:** Adam's family line to Noah, with its refrain "and he died". Enoch walks with God and is taken.
- **Genesis 6:** Human wickedness fills the earth and God resolves to send a flood. Noah, who finds favour with God, is told to build an ark, and God promises his covenant (6:18).
- **Genesis 7:** Noah, his family and the animals enter the ark. The flood covers the earth and every living thing outside the ark dies.
- **Genesis 8:** The waters go down; Noah sends out a raven and a dove and leaves the ark. He offers sacrifice, and God promises never again to curse the ground this way (8:21–22).
- **Genesis 9:** God makes his covenant with Noah, with the rainbow as its sign, and forbids murder because humans bear his image (9:6). Noah gets drunk, and Canaan is cursed.
- **Genesis 10:** The table of nations: the peoples descended from Shem, Ham and Japheth.
- **Genesis 11:** At Babel people try to make a name for themselves, and God confuses their language. Shem's line leads to Terah and Abram, whose wife Sarai is barren.
- **Genesis 12:** God calls Abram to leave home and promises land, offspring, and blessing for all families of the earth (12:1–3). In Egypt Abram passes Sarai off as his sister.
- **Genesis 13:** Abram and Lot separate; Lot chooses the land near Sodom. God renews the land promise.
- **Genesis 14:** Abram rescues Lot from invading kings. Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, blesses Abram, who gives him a tenth.
- **Genesis 15:** Abram believes God's promise, and God counts it to him as righteousness (15:6). God alone passes between the cut animals to seal the covenant, and foretells slavery and the exodus.
- **Genesis 16:** Sarai gives Hagar to Abram, and Ishmael is born. Hagar meets "the God who sees" her.
- **Genesis 17:** God gives the covenant sign of circumcision and renames Abram and Sarai as Abraham and Sarah. He promises Isaac.
- **Genesis 18:** Three visitors come to Abraham at Mamre; Sarah laughs at the promise of a son. Abraham pleads with God for Sodom.
- **Genesis 19:** Sodom and Gomorrah are destroyed. Lot is rescued, his wife looks back, and his daughters' sons father Moab and Ammon.
- **Genesis 20:** Abraham again calls Sarah his sister, this time to Abimelech of Gerar, and God protects her.
- **Genesis 21:** Isaac is born. Hagar and Ishmael are sent away, and God provides for them in the wilderness.
- **Genesis 22:** God tests Abraham by telling him to offer Isaac. He stops him and provides a ram: "The Lord will provide" (22:14).
- **Genesis 23:** Sarah dies, and Abraham buys the cave of Machpelah to bury her, his first piece of the promised land.
- **Genesis 24:** Abraham's servant finds Rebekah in Mesopotamia as a wife for Isaac, guided by God's answer to prayer.
- **Genesis 25:** Abraham dies. Rebekah bears twins, Esau and Jacob, and Esau sells his birthright for stew.
- **Genesis 26:** God renews the covenant promises to Isaac. Isaac repeats his father's "she is my sister" lie and digs wells in Gerar.
- **Genesis 27:** With Rebekah's help, Jacob deceives his blind father and takes Esau's blessing. Esau plans to kill him.
- **Genesis 28:** Jacob flees. At Bethel he dreams of a stairway to heaven, God repeats the covenant promises to him, and Jacob makes a vow.
- **Genesis 29:** Jacob works seven years for Rachel but is tricked into marrying Leah, then works seven more for Rachel. Leah bears Reuben, Simeon, Levi and Judah.
- **Genesis 30:** Rivalry between the sisters brings more sons through their servants. Rachel finally bears Joseph, and Jacob's flocks grow.
- **Genesis 31:** Jacob leaves Laban secretly; Rachel steals her father's household gods. Jacob and Laban part with a covenant at Mizpah.
- **Genesis 32:** Afraid of meeting Esau, Jacob prays and sends gifts. He wrestles with God at Peniel and is renamed Israel.
- **Genesis 33:** Esau welcomes Jacob, and they are reconciled; Jacob presses gifts on him, as if handing back the blessing. Jacob settles near Shechem.
- **Genesis 34:** Dinah is violated by Shechem, and her brothers Simeon and Levi take bloody revenge on the city.
- **Genesis 35:** Jacob returns to Bethel and God renews the covenant. Rachel dies giving birth to Benjamin, and Isaac dies.
- **Genesis 36:** Esau leaves the promised land, yet is blessed with children, wealth and land: the line of the Edomites.
- **Genesis 37:** Joseph, Jacob's favourite son, dreams of ruling his brothers. They sell him to traders bound for Egypt.
- **Genesis 38:** Judah wrongs his daughter-in-law Tamar, who bears twins by him, Perez and Zerah. God uses even this to carry on the promised line: Perez is an ancestor of David and of Jesus, and Judah begins to change.
- **Genesis 39:** Joseph serves Potiphar faithfully, refuses his wife, and is falsely accused and jailed. In every place "the Lord was with Joseph".
- **Genesis 40:** In prison Joseph interprets the dreams of Pharaoh's cupbearer and baker.
- **Genesis 41:** Joseph interprets Pharaoh's dreams of seven good years and seven years of famine, and is made ruler over Egypt.
- **Genesis 42:** Joseph's brothers come to buy grain. He recognises them, tests them, and keeps Simeon.
- **Genesis 43:** The brothers return with Benjamin and eat with Joseph, who still hides who he is.
- **Genesis 44:** Joseph's silver cup is planted in Benjamin's sack, and Judah offers himself as a slave in Benjamin's place.
- **Genesis 45:** Joseph reveals himself: God sent him ahead to save lives (45:5–8). He sends for his father.
- **Genesis 46:** God tells Jacob not to fear going down to Egypt, and the family of seventy goes.
- **Genesis 47:** Jacob's family settles in Goshen, and Jacob blesses Pharaoh. Joseph's handling of the famine feeds the hungry and strengthens Pharaoh's hold on Egypt.
- **Genesis 48:** Jacob adopts and blesses Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh, putting the younger first.
- **Genesis 49:** Jacob blesses his twelve sons. The sceptre will not depart from Judah (49:10). Jacob dies.
- **Genesis 50:** Jacob is buried in Canaan. Joseph reassures his brothers: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (50:20). Joseph dies, making Israel promise to carry his bones to the land.

## Key people

- **Adam and Eve:** the first humans, made in God's image; their disobedience brings sin and death into the world.
- **Cain and Abel:** Adam's sons; Cain murders Abel out of jealousy.
- **Noah:** a righteous man who walks with God; saved through the flood with his family.
- **Abraham (Abram):** called from Ur and Haran, given God's covenant promises, and counted righteous by faith (15:6). Father of Israel and "father of all who believe" (Rom 4:11).
- **Sarah (Sarai):** Abraham's wife; barren until God gives Isaac in her old age.
- **Lot:** Abraham's nephew, rescued from Sodom.
- **Melchizedek:** king of Salem and priest of God Most High, who blesses Abraham.
- **Hagar and Ishmael:** Sarah's Egyptian servant and her son by Abraham.
- **Isaac:** the promised son, nearly offered on Mount Moriah; heir of the covenant.
- **Rebekah:** Isaac's wife, mother of Esau and Jacob.
- **Esau:** Isaac's older son, who despises his birthright; ancestor of Edom.
- **Jacob (Israel):** a schemer whom God chooses, renames, and makes father of the twelve tribes.
- **Rachel and Leah:** Jacob's wives, sisters and rivals.
- **Judah:** Jacob's son who sells Joseph, wrongs Tamar, and later offers himself as a slave in Benjamin's place; the sceptre is promised to his tribe (49:10).
- **Tamar and Perez:** Judah's daughter-in-law and their son, through whom the royal line of David runs (38; Ruth 4:18–22).
- **Joseph:** Jacob's favourite son, sold into slavery and raised to rule Egypt, saving his family. His story fills more than a fifth of the book's chapters (*RH*[^rh]). His son Ephraim is blessed ahead of the older Manasseh (48).

## Key events

- Creation in six days; humanity made in God's image (1–2)
- The fall, and the first promise of a Saver (3:15)
- Cain murders Abel (4)
- The flood, and God's covenant with Noah (6–9)
- The tower of Babel (11)
- God calls Abram (12:1–3)
- Abram is counted righteous by faith; the covenant ceremony (15)
- The covenant sign of circumcision; Isaac promised (17)
- Sodom and Gomorrah destroyed (19)
- Isaac born (21); Abraham's test on Mount Moriah (22)
- Jacob takes Esau's birthright and blessing (25; 27)
- Jacob's dream at Bethel (28); he wrestles with God and becomes Israel (32)
- Joseph sold into Egypt (37); raised to power (41)
- Joseph reveals himself; Jacob's family moves to Egypt (45–46)
- Jacob's blessings, and the sceptre promised to Judah (49)

## Themes

- **God the Creator, above and near:** God speaks and the world is ordered, and everything he makes is good (1:1–2:3). Yet he also forms the man by hand and walks with him in the garden (2:4–3:24). The *RSB*[^rsb] notes how the two accounts show his transcendence and his closeness.
- **Humanity as God's rulers:** made in his image to fill the earth and rule it under him (1:26–28).
- **Sin and its spread:** one act of disobedience leads to shame, murder, revenge, violence, the flood and Babel's "anti-kingdom" (3; 4; 6:5; 11). "The intention of man's heart is evil from his youth" (8:21).
- **Promise and offspring:** the "offspring" of 3:15 runs through the genealogies to Abraham's offspring (12:7; 22:18). By the end two royal lines stand out, Joseph's and Judah's, and Judah's wins (49:8–10), pointing to David.
- **God's sovereign choice:** God chooses Abraham, and repeatedly the younger over the older: Isaac, Jacob, Perez, Ephraim (25:23; 48). He opens barren wombs and protects the mothers of the line (12; 20; 21).
- **Covenant:** God binds himself to Noah, Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, initiating and guaranteeing each covenant himself (6:18; 9:9; 15; 17; 26:3–5; 28:13–15). *RH*[^rh] contrasts Adam, set under a law of obedience with the two trees as its signs (2:9, 15–17), with Abraham's family, who stand by grace and are counted righteous by faith, not by works (15:6).
- **Faith and righteousness:** Abraham believes God, and it is counted to him as righteousness (15:6). Luther called Genesis an "exceedingly evangelical" book, full of examples of faith and unbelief (*LSB*[^lsb]).
- **Faith tested by providence:** God's people must trust the promise when events seem to deny it: Abel dies for his faith, Abraham waits decades for one son and never owns the land, Jacob spends years in exile, and Joseph is enslaved. Yet barren wives, family deceit and slavery all serve God's plan: "God meant it for good" (50:20).
- **Blessing for the nations:** God's purpose with Abraham's family is blessing for "all the families of the earth" (12:3).

## How it points to Christ

The first gospel promise is the offspring of the woman who will crush the serpent's head, at the cost of a wound to his heel (3:15; Rom 16:20). Abraham's "offspring" is ultimately one person, Christ (Gal 3:16). The garments of skin God makes after the fall (3:21) and Abel's offering (4:4) begin the line of sacrifices that foreshadow Christ, the one perfect sacrifice (*RH*[^rh]). Melchizedek, priest-king of Salem, foreshadows Christ's priesthood (Heb 7). The binding of Isaac points to the Father who did not spare his own Son (Rom 8:32; Heb 11:17–19). Jacob's stairway to heaven is fulfilled in Jesus himself (John 1:51). The sceptre promised to Judah (49:10) belongs to the Lion of the tribe of Judah (Rev 5:5).

Joseph, rejected by his brothers and raised to save many lives, is a pattern of Christ's suffering and exaltation. Adam himself is "a type of the one who was to come" (Rom 5:14).

## Common mix-ups

- The serpent deceives Eve, but the New Testament holds Adam responsible for sin entering the world (Rom 5:12).
- Abraham's covenant is in chapters 15 and 17, and his test with Isaac is chapter 22; don't confuse it with the covenant ceremony.
- "She is my sister" happens three times: Abraham in Egypt (12) and in Gerar (20), and Isaac in Gerar (26).
- Jacob is renamed Israel at Peniel (32:28), and God confirms the name at Bethel (35:10).
- Joseph is sold by his brothers to traders (Midianites and Ishmaelites, 37:25–28), not by Potiphar.
- The sceptre goes to Judah, not to Joseph: Joseph receives the double portion through Ephraim and Manasseh.
- Moses' story begins in Exodus; Genesis ends with Joseph's death.

## Going deeper

- **How are the creation days to be read?** Luther insisted Moses spoke literally of six days (*LSB*[^lsb]). The Westminster Confession says God made the world "in the space of six days" (4.1). The *LSB*[^lsb] warns against dating creation by adding up the genealogies, since they may skip generations. *RH*[^rh] reads the days as ordinary days, because "day" with "evening and morning" always means that elsewhere in the Old Testament. It argues that denying a historical Adam undoes the unity of mankind, Christ as the last Adam, and death as the penalty for sin (Rom 5:12; 1 Cor 15:21–22). Discuss what Genesis 1 does and doesn't claim.
- **Did Moses write Genesis?** Currid[^bto] traces the theory that Genesis was stitched together from several source documents back to Jean Astruc and Hegel's philosophy of development. He argues that repeated key words and patterns show one artful author. Read: Currid[^bto], Background Issues and Literary Analysis.
- **The two seeds.** Currid[^bto] follows the conflict of 3:15 through Cain and Seth, the flood, and Abraham's line, all the way to Revelation 12. Who belongs to which "offspring"?
- **Covenant in Genesis.** God's covenants follow the ancient pattern of a great king binding himself to a lesser one. In Genesis 15 only God passes between the pieces, taking the curse on himself (Currid[^bto], Centrality of the Covenant). Connect this with the covenant of grace (WCF 7.3).
- **Genesis and ancient myths.** Babylonian flood stories such as the Gilgamesh epic have striking parallels. Currid[^bto] argues Genesis uses familiar forms to make polemical, God-centred claims. Read: Currid[^bto], excursus on Genesis 1–11.
- **Is the patriarchs' world historical?** Currid's[^bto] excursus on archaeology points to Middle Bronze Age cities, nomadic herding and Egyptian customs (Joseph's sale for twenty shekels, prisons) that fit the stories.

## Q&A

1. **Q:** What do the book's Greek and Hebrew titles mean?

   **A:** The Greek title, Genesis, means "origin"; the Hebrew title is its first words, "In the beginning" (1:1).

2. **Q:** What is the first gospel promise in the Bible?

   **A:** Genesis 3:15: the woman's offspring will crush the serpent's head.

3. **Q:** What was the sign of God's covenant with Noah?

   **A:** The rainbow (9:13).

4. **Q:** What three things did God promise Abram in Genesis 12?

   **A:** Land, offspring (a great nation), and blessing for all the families of the earth (12:1–3).

5. **Q:** Which verse says Abraham was counted righteous by faith?

   **A:** Genesis 15:6.

6. **Q:** What did God provide in place of Isaac?

   **A:** A ram caught in a thicket (22:13).

7. **Q:** Who bought Esau's birthright, and for what?

   **A:** Jacob, for a bowl of stew (25:29–34).

8. **Q:** Where did Jacob dream of the stairway to heaven?

   **A:** Bethel (28:19).

9. **Q:** What was Jacob renamed, and when?

   **A:** Israel, after wrestling with God at Peniel (32:28).

10. **Q:** Which son received the promise of the sceptre?

    **A:** Judah (49:10).

11. **Q:** How did Joseph explain his brothers' betrayal?

    **A:** "You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (50:20).
