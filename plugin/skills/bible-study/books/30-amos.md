# Amos

## Summary

Amos, a shepherd and fig farmer from Judah, is sent north to prosperous Israel to announce that God will judge it. The rich trample the poor, bribe judges, and pile up luxuries while keeping busy religious festivals. God hates their worship without justice: "let justice roll down like waters" (5:24). Through visions Amos sees the end coming, but the book closes with hope: God will rebuild David's fallen house and restore his people.

## Setting

- **When:** in the reigns of Uzziah of Judah and Jeroboam II of Israel, "two years before the earthquake" (1:1), about 760 BC (the Lutheran Study Bible gives about 792–740 BC). It was a time of peace and wealth for Israel.
- **Where:** Amos was from Tekoa in Judah but preached in the northern kingdom, at Bethel and Samaria.
- **Who wrote it:** Amos, who insists he was no professional prophet but a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore figs whom God called (7:14–15).
- **Where it sits:** among the Twelve. Timmer dates Amos with Hosea to the mid-8th century, the start of the Twelve's rough chronological span.

## Structure

- 1–2: Oracles against the nations, closing in on Judah and finally Israel
- 3–6: Messages against Israel: privilege and responsibility, oppression, empty worship, complacency
- 7:1–9:10: Five visions of judgment, with Amos' confrontation with Amaziah
- 9:11–15: Restoration of David's fallen booth

## Chapter by chapter

- **Amos 1:** "For three transgressions … and for four", God will punish Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom and Ammon for their cruelty.
- **Amos 2:** Moab, then Judah for rejecting God's law, then Israel itself for selling the righteous for silver and trampling the poor.
- **Amos 3:** "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you" (3:2). When the lion roars, the prophet must speak.
- **Amos 4:** The rich women of Samaria, "cows of Bashan", are condemned. God sent famine, drought and plague, "yet you did not return to me". "Prepare to meet your God, O Israel" (4:12).
- **Amos 5:** A funeral lament for Israel. "Seek me and live" (5:4). The day of the Lord will be darkness. God hates their feasts: "let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (5:24).
- **Amos 6:** Woe to those at ease in Zion and secure in Samaria, lounging on ivory beds while their nation falls apart.
- **Amos 7:** Visions of locusts and fire: Amos intercedes and God relents. Then a plumb line: judgment is set. Amaziah the priest of Bethel orders Amos home, and Amos answers that God called him from following the flock.
- **Amos 8:** A basket of summer fruit means the end has come. Merchants can't wait for the Sabbath to cheat again. A famine is coming, not of bread, but of hearing the words of the Lord (8:11).
- **Amos 9:** The Lord stands by the altar and strikes. No one can hide from him. Yet God will raise up the fallen booth of David, and the ploughman will overtake the reaper as the land overflows with blessing.

## Key people

- **Amos:** a shepherd and farmer from Tekoa, called to prophesy to Israel.
- **Jeroboam II:** king of Israel in a time of prosperity.
- **Uzziah:** king of Judah at the time.
- **Amaziah:** the priest of Bethel who tells Amos to go home (7:10–17).
- **The "cows of Bashan":** the wealthy women of Samaria who oppress the poor (4:1).

## Key events

- The oracles against the nations (1–2)
- The visions of locusts, fire and the plumb line (7:1–9)
- Amaziah's confrontation with Amos (7:10–17)
- The vision of summer fruit (8:1–3)
- The promise to restore David's booth (9:11–15)

## Themes

- **Social justice as covenant faithfulness:** God condemns oppression of the poor, bribery, cheating and exploitation (2:6–8; 5:11–12; 8:4–6).
- **Worship without justice is hated:** God rejects feasts and songs from people who trample the needy (5:21–24).
- **Privilege brings responsibility:** Israel's special relationship with God makes its sin worse (3:2).
- **God's judgment on all nations:** the God of Israel holds every nation accountable for its cruelty (1–2).
- **Seek the Lord and live:** Timmer notes how Amos ties repentance to "life" (5:4, 6, 14), and how it reaches beyond a single historical judgment.
- **Hope for a remnant:** God will not utterly destroy Jacob, and will restore David's house (9:8, 11–15).

## How it points to Christ

At the Jerusalem council, James quoted Amos 9:11–12 to show that God's rebuilding of David's fallen tent includes the Gentiles who are called by his name. The risen Son of David gathers a people from all nations (Acts 15:13–18). Stephen quoted Amos 5:25–27 to show Israel's long history of resisting God (Acts 7:42–43).

Jesus, like Amos, confronted religious leaders who neglected "justice and mercy and faithfulness" (Matt 23:23), and he brings the true restoration Amos foresaw.

## Common mix-ups

- Amos was from the southern kingdom (Tekoa in Judah) but prophesied mainly to the northern kingdom.
- Amos wasn't a professional prophet; he was a herdsman and fig grower (7:14).
- "Let justice roll down like waters" is Amos 5:24, famously quoted by Martin Luther King Jr.
- "Prepare to meet your God" (4:12) is a warning of judgment, not an evangelistic slogan.
- The "booth" or "tent" of David (9:11) means David's fallen dynasty, not a building.

## Going deeper

- **The rhetorical trap of chapters 1–2.** Amos condemns Israel's neighbours, then Judah, then springs the trap on Israel itself. How would his hearers have felt? What does that suggest about how we hear judgment on others?
- **Justice and worship.** Why does God reject worship from people who mistreat the poor (5:21–24)? Compare Isaiah 1:10–17 and James 2:14–17.
- **Intercession and God relenting (7:1–6).** God relents twice at Amos' prayer, then sets the plumb line. What does this teach about prayer and God's purposes?
- **A famine of the word (8:11–12).** What does it mean for God to withhold his word as judgment?
- **The restored booth of David (9:11–15) and Acts 15.** How did James read Amos to include the Gentiles? Timmer discusses the deliverance of some among the nations in the Twelve.

## Q&A

1. What was Amos' job before God called him? A herdsman and dresser of sycamore figs (7:14).
2. Where was Amos from, and where did he preach? From Tekoa in Judah; he preached in the northern kingdom of Israel.
3. How does Amos introduce his oracles against the nations? "For three transgressions … and for four" (1:3).
4. Why does God punish Israel more because it was chosen? "You only have I known … therefore I will punish you" (3:2).
5. What did Amos call the wealthy women of Samaria? "Cows of Bashan" (4:1).
6. What should roll down like waters? Justice, with righteousness like an ever-flowing stream (5:24).
7. What did the plumb line vision mean? That God had measured Israel and found it crooked, so judgment was set (7:7–9).
8. Who told Amos to go back to Judah? Amaziah, the priest of Bethel (7:10–13).
9. What kind of famine did Amos predict? A famine of hearing the words of the Lord (8:11).
10. Which promise from Amos did James quote at the Jerusalem council? The rebuilding of David's fallen booth (9:11–12; Acts 15:16–17).
