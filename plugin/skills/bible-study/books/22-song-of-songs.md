## Summary

The Song of Solomon (also called the Song of Songs, "the greatest song") is a collection of love poetry celebrating the love between a woman and her beloved. In vivid images of gardens, vineyards and spices, it portrays desire, longing, separation and reunion, and it insists on love's commitment and power: "love is strong as death … many waters cannot quench love" (8:6–7). As wisdom literature, it teaches God's people how to think about love and marriage in God's world.

## Setting

- **When:** traditionally Solomon's reign (about 970–930 BC). Van Pelt notes two options: Solomon wrote it in the 10th century BC, or an unknown author wrote it between the 10th and 5th centuries BC.
- **Where:** Jerusalem and the countryside of Israel: vineyards, gardens, Lebanon, Engedi, Sharon.
- **Who wrote it:** the heading reads "The Song of Songs, which is Solomon's" (1:1). That can mean written by, for, or about Solomon.
- **Where it sits:** among the Writings and the wisdom books (Van Pelt). In Jewish tradition it is the first of the five festival scrolls, read at Passover.

## Structure

Interpreters range from seeing many loosely connected poems to one unified drama. Van Pelt finds four main sections. Each ends with the woman putting the "daughters of Jerusalem" under oath not to stir up love before its time (2:7; 3:5; 8:4), and each new section begins with someone arriving (2:8; 3:6; 8:5):

- 1:1–2:7: Opening longing; temptation from Solomon's court
- 2:8–3:5: The beloved comes; springtime invitation; night search
- 3:6–8:4: Solomon's procession, praise poems, separation and search, and longing
- 8:5–14: The woman arrives with her beloved; the climax on love's strength (8:6–7); the vineyard

## Chapter by chapter

- **Song 1:** The title. The woman longs for her beloved's love, says she is "dark, but lovely" from working in the vineyards, and asks where he pastures his flock. The lovers praise each other.
- **Song 2:** "I am a rose of Sharon". Her beloved comes leaping over the hills and invites her out into the spring: "Arise, my love". "Catch the foxes" that spoil the vineyards. "My beloved is mine, and I am his" (2:16).
- **Song 3:** At night she searches the city for her beloved and finds him. A grand procession of Solomon approaches on his carriage, on his wedding day.
- **Song 4:** A detailed poem praising the woman's beauty, and "a garden locked", a picture of her purity, opened to her beloved.
- **Song 5:** In a dream-like scene her beloved knocks, she hesitates, and when she opens he is gone. She searches, is mistreated by the watchmen, and describes her beloved to the daughters of Jerusalem.
- **Song 6:** He has gone down to his garden. He praises her as unique among all women, "awesome as an army with banners".
- **Song 7:** Another poem praising her beauty, and her desire for him: "I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me" (7:10).
- **Song 8:** Longing for open closeness. "Set me as a seal upon your heart, … for love is strong as death" (8:6). Many waters can't quench it. Solomon may keep his vineyard, but her own vineyard is hers to give (8:11–12).

## Key people

- **The woman (the Shulammite, 6:13):** the main voice of the Song, who speaks most of its lines.
- **The beloved:** her lover, often pictured as a shepherd (1:7; 2:16).
- **Solomon:** named several times (1:1, 5; 3:7–11; 8:11–12). In the traditional reading he is the beloved himself. In Van Pelt's reading he is a rival who tries to win her into his harem, and whom she rejects.
- **The daughters of Jerusalem:** a group the woman speaks to and warns (2:7; 3:5; 5:8; 8:4).
- **Her brothers:** who once set her to work in the vineyards (1:6; 8:8–9).

## Key events

The Song is poetry, not a straightforward story, but it includes:

- The woman's longing and the lovers' praise of each other (1)
- The beloved's springtime invitation (2:8–17)
- The night search and finding (3:1–5)
- Solomon's wedding procession (3:6–11)
- The second night search (5:2–8)
- The climactic poem on love's strength (8:6–7)

## Themes

- **The goodness of married love:** God made human love and desire, and they are good in their proper place (Gen 2:23–25). The Song celebrates them without embarrassment.
- **Love's commitment and power:** Van Pelt finds the book's message in 8:6–10: love in God's design for marriage is both steadfast and passionate. It endures hardship, resists temptation and brings wholeness.
- **Don't awaken love too soon:** the repeated oath (2:7; 3:5; 8:4) urges patience and restraint until the right time.
- **Exclusive belonging:** "My beloved is mine, and I am his" (2:16; 6:3; 7:10).
- **Love can't be bought:** wealth can't purchase true love (8:7), and the woman refuses Solomon's vineyard for her own (8:11–12).
- **Wisdom for life:** Van Pelt reads the Song as wisdom teaching, showing how to make good decisions about love in God's world.

## How it points to Christ

Scripture uses marriage to picture God's covenant love for his people (Isa 54:5; Hos 2:19–20). Paul says marriage is ultimately about Christ and the church (Eph 5:25–32). So even read as a song about human love, the Song points beyond itself to the greater love of the Bridegroom for his bride, a love truly "strong as death", which went to the cross (John 15:13) and ends in the marriage supper of the Lamb (Rev 19:7–9).

Much of the church's history, including many Puritans, read the Song directly as an allegory of Christ and his church. Van Pelt takes a different approach: the Song is first wisdom about human love and marriage, and the gospel connection runs through marriage itself as God designed it.

## Common mix-ups

- The book has three common names: Song of Solomon, Song of Songs, and Canticles.
- "Song of Songs" means "the greatest song", as "King of kings" means the greatest king.
- God is named directly at most once, in "the very flame of the Lord" (8:6, in some translations). The Song is still Scripture: it teaches about God's good gift of love.
- "I am the rose of Sharon" (2:1) is spoken by the woman, not the man, though hymns often apply it to Christ.
- Interpreters disagree whether there are two main characters (Solomon and his bride) or three (the woman, her shepherd beloved, and Solomon as rival). Van Pelt takes the three-character view.
- The Song isn't only for married couples or only about sex: it is wisdom about love, including patience and restraint before marriage.

## Going deeper

- **How should the Song be read?** Compare three approaches:
    - **Allegory:** Christ and the church, the dominant reading for most of church history.
    - **Love poetry:** a celebration of human love and marriage.
    - **Wisdom:** Van Pelt's reading, teaching about love and marriage.

  Read: Van Pelt, Interpretation.
- **Two characters or three?** Van Pelt argues the setting is Solomon's harem, with the woman resisting Solomon for her beloved. Test it against 3:6–11 and 8:11–12. What does each view do with Solomon?
- **"Do not stir up or awaken love until it pleases" (2:7).** What does this refrain teach young people about desire, timing and self-control?
- **Love strong as death (8:6–7).** What makes covenant love "unquenchable"? How does Christ's love fulfil it (Rom 8:35–39)?
- **The garden imagery.** Gardens appear throughout (4:12–5:1; 6:2, 11). How might the Song echo Eden and the goodness of creation (Gen 2)?

## Q&A

1. What are the other names for this book? Song of Songs and Canticles.
2. What does "Song of Songs" mean? The greatest or most excellent song.
3. To whom does the woman address her repeated charge? The daughters of Jerusalem (2:7; 3:5; 8:4).
4. What is the charge? Not to stir up or awaken love until it pleases.
5. Who says "I am a rose of Sharon"? The woman (2:1).
6. What phrase expresses the lovers' mutual belonging? "My beloved is mine, and I am his" (2:16).
7. What happens when the woman delays opening the door to her beloved? He has gone, and she searches for him (5:2–8).
8. What does 8:6 say love is as strong as? Death.
9. What can't quench love, according to 8:7? Many waters, or floods.
10. How does the New Testament connect marriage with Christ? Paul says marriage refers to Christ and the church (Eph 5:31–32).
