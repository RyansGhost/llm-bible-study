# Daniel

## Summary

Daniel shows God's people how to live faithfully in exile, confident that God rules over the kings of the earth. In the first half, Daniel and his friends stay loyal to God in Babylon's court, and God rescues them from a fiery furnace and a lions' den while humbling proud kings. In the second half, Daniel's visions reveal a succession of world empires, intense persecution, and the final triumph of God's kingdom, given to "one like a son of man" (7:13–14).

## Setting

- **When:** Daniel was taken to Babylon as a young man in 605 BC, after Babylon defeated Egypt at Carchemish. His ministry lasted until after Cyrus' conquest of Babylon in 539 BC (1:21; 10:1) (Belcher).
- **Where:** the royal court of Babylon, and then of Persia.
- **Who wrote it:** Daniel, whose name means "God is my Judge". The book presents him as its author (9:2; 10:2), written soon after 539 BC, and Jesus quotes "the prophet Daniel" (Matt 24:15). Many critical scholars date it to about 165 BC, during Antiochus IV's persecution, because its prophecies of that period (11) are so detailed. The Reformation Study Bible answers that this rests mainly on assuming predictive prophecy is impossible. The other arguments don't require a late date either: Belshazzar and "Darius the Mede" have reasonable explanations, the later-looking Aramaic may reflect updating by copyists, and a few Greek words for instruments fit early contact with Greece.
- **Where it sits:** among the Major Prophets in English Bibles, and among the Writings in the Hebrew Bible. Belcher notes it is written in Hebrew (1:1–2:4a; 8–12) and Aramaic (2:4b–7:28). The Aramaic section concerns the nations, the Hebrew section God's people in particular.

## Structure

The basic division (Belcher; Reformation Study Bible):

- 1–6: Stories of Daniel and his friends at the Babylonian and Persian courts, faithful in exile (compare Jer 29:4–7)
- 7–12: Daniel's apocalyptic visions of the future

Both halves carry one message: Nebuchadnezzar's dream (2) and Daniel's vision (7) say the same thing, that God rules history and will rescue his people from or through their trials. Belcher also pairs chapters by theme:

- 1: living faithfully in exile
- 2 and 7: four kingdoms, and the victory of God's kingdom
- 3 and 6: faithfulness even unto death
- 4 and 5: God rules over proud kings
- 8 and 10–12: faithfulness under intense persecution
- 9: looking for the completion of salvation (the seventy weeks)

## Chapter by chapter

- **Daniel 1:** Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah are taken to Babylon, renamed (Belteshazzar, Shadrach, Meshach, Abednego) and trained for royal service. They refuse the king's food and thrive on vegetables, and God gives them wisdom.
- **Daniel 2:** Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a statue of gold, silver, bronze, iron and clay. God reveals the dream and its meaning to Daniel: four kingdoms, then a stone cut without hands that becomes a mountain, God's everlasting kingdom.
- **Daniel 3:** Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego refuse to bow to a gold image. Thrown into the furnace, they are unharmed, and a fourth figure walks with them.
- **Daniel 4:** Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a great tree cut down. In his pride he loses his mind and lives like an animal, until he acknowledges that the Most High rules, and is restored.
- **Daniel 5:** At Belshazzar's drunken feast with the temple vessels, a hand writes on the wall: "Mene, Mene, Tekel, Parsin". Daniel interprets it, and that night Babylon falls.
- **Daniel 6:** Officials trick Darius into banning prayer to anyone but the king. Daniel keeps praying, is thrown to the lions, and God shuts their mouths.
- **Daniel 7:** Daniel sees four beasts rise from the sea. The Ancient of Days sits in judgment, and one like a son of man comes with the clouds and receives an everlasting kingdom.
- **Daniel 8:** A ram (Medo-Persia) and a goat (Greece). A little horn grows proud and defiles the sanctuary. Gabriel explains.
- **Daniel 9:** Reading Jeremiah's seventy years, Daniel confesses Israel's sin. Gabriel reveals "seventy weeks", in which an anointed one is cut off.
- **Daniel 10:** By the Tigris, Daniel sees a man clothed in linen and learns of conflict in the heavens with "the prince of Persia", in which Michael helps.
- **Daniel 11:** A detailed prophecy of wars between the kings of the north and south, and a contemptible king who profanes the sanctuary.
- **Daniel 12:** A time of unequalled trouble, Michael standing up, and the resurrection: many will awake, some to everlasting life and some to shame (12:2). Daniel is told to seal the book, rest, and rise to his reward.

## Key people

- **Daniel (Belteshazzar):** a young exile from Judah, wise and faithful, who serves in the courts of Babylon and Persia and receives visions.
- **Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego (Hananiah, Mishael, Azariah):** Daniel's friends, rescued from the furnace.
- **Nebuchadnezzar:** king of Babylon, humbled and brought to confess God.
- **Belshazzar:** the last ruler of Babylon, who misuses the temple vessels.
- **Darius the Mede:** the ruler who throws Daniel to the lions.
- **Cyrus:** king of Persia, in whose time Daniel's ministry ends.
- **Gabriel:** the angel who explains Daniel's visions (8; 9).
- **Michael:** "the great prince" who protects God's people (10; 12).
- **The Ancient of Days:** God, enthroned in judgment (7).
- **One like a son of man:** the figure who receives God's everlasting kingdom (7:13–14).

## Themes

- **Faithfulness in exile:** Belcher frames the whole book this way: how to serve God faithfully in a hostile culture without compromise (1; 3; 6).
- **God rules over earthly kings:** "the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will" (4:17). Proud kings are humbled (4–5).
- **The kingdoms of the world and the kingdom of God:** empires rise and fall, but God's kingdom will never be destroyed (2:44; 7:14). Many who hold to the early date read the four kingdoms of 2 and 7 as Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome; critics who take them as ending with Greece must posit a separate Median empire that never existed between Babylon and Persia (RSB).
- **Suffering and persecution:** God's people will face intense persecution (7:21–25; 8; 11), but it is limited and will end. Antiochus IV "Epiphanes" (175–164 BC) tried to stamp out Jewish faith, and Daniel strengthened those who resisted him, as it did Christians under Rome. Beyond him, Daniel points to a final enemy, whom the New Testament calls the man of lawlessness, and a "time of trouble" before the end (11:36–12:1; Matt 24:15; 2 Thess 2:3–4).
- **Prayer and confession:** Daniel prays regularly (6:10) and confesses his people's sin, appealing to God's mercy (9:3–19).
- **Resurrection and final judgment:** Daniel 12:2 is the Old Testament's clearest statement of bodily resurrection.

## How it points to Christ

The stone "cut out by no human hand" that crushes the empires and fills the earth (2:34–45) is the kingdom Christ brings. Jesus speaks of himself as the stone that crushes (Luke 20:17–18). His favourite title for himself, "the Son of Man", comes from Daniel 7:13–14. Before the high priest he claimed to be that one, coming on the clouds of heaven (Mark 14:61–62), and he was given all authority (Matt 28:18).

Many interpreters see Christ's death in the "anointed one" who is "cut off" in the seventy weeks (9:24–27), bringing in everlasting righteousness. Jesus referred to Daniel's "abomination of desolation" (Matt 24:15). Daniel's resurrection hope (12:2) is echoed in Jesus' words about the hour when the dead will hear his voice (John 5:28–29), and Revelation draws heavily on Daniel's visions of beasts and the Son of Man (Rev 1:13–14; 13).

## Common mix-ups

- Daniel was in the lions' den under Darius the Mede, not Nebuchadnezzar. The fiery furnace was under Nebuchadnezzar, and Daniel wasn't in it.
- "Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego" are Babylonian names. Their Hebrew names are Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah.
- The writing on the wall appears at Belshazzar's feast (5), the night Babylon fell to the Medes and Persians.
- Daniel was taken to Babylon in 605 BC, before Ezekiel (597 BC) and before Jerusalem's fall (586 BC).
- Part of Daniel is in Aramaic (2:4b–7:28), not Hebrew.
- The additions in Greek Bibles (the Prayer of Azariah, Susanna, Bel and the Dragon) are not in the Hebrew Bible, and Protestants don't treat them as Scripture.
- Interpreters disagree whether the fourth man in the furnace (3:25) is an angel or a pre-incarnate appearance of the Son.

## Going deeper

- **Living faithfully in exile.** Belcher reads Daniel as a guide for God's people in a foreign culture. What did Daniel and his friends accept (new names, education, service) and refuse (food, idol worship, giving up prayer)? Compare 1 Peter 1:1 and 2:11–12, which Belcher connects to Christians' own exile.
- **The four kingdoms.** Daniel 2 and 7 cover the same history. The traditional view reads Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome. Others read Babylon, Media, Persia and Greece. What is at stake? Read: Belcher on Daniel 2 and 7.
- **The seventy weeks (9:24–27).** Belcher sets out the debates over the starting point and over how to translate 9:25. Consider the main approaches: messianic (fulfilled in Christ), focused on Antiochus, or dispensational (a future gap).
- **Antiochus IV and beyond.** Belcher shows how chapters 8 and 11 match the persecution of 170–164 BC in detail, while also pointing beyond it to the end and the resurrection (12).
- **Daniel's prayer (9:3–19).** A model of confession grounded in God's word (Jeremiah) and appealing to God's mercy, not Israel's righteousness.
- **Spiritual warfare (10).** What does the "prince of Persia" passage reveal about unseen powers behind earthly empires? Compare Ephesians 6:12.

## Q&A

1. **Q:** When was Daniel taken to Babylon?

   **A:** In 605 BC, under Nebuchadnezzar (1:1).

2. **Q:** What did Daniel and his friends refuse?

   **A:** The king's food and wine (1:8).

3. **Q:** What did Nebuchadnezzar's statue dream show?

   **A:** Four kingdoms, and a stone that becomes God's everlasting kingdom (2:31–45).

4. **Q:** Who was thrown into the fiery furnace?

   **A:** Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego, and a fourth figure was seen walking with them (3:19–25).

5. **Q:** Why was Nebuchadnezzar made to live like an animal?

   **A:** Because of his pride, until he acknowledged that the Most High rules (4:30–37).

6. **Q:** What did the writing on the wall mean?

   **A:** Belshazzar had been weighed and found wanting, and his kingdom given to the Medes and Persians (5:25–28).

7. **Q:** Why was Daniel thrown into the lions' den?

   **A:** He kept praying to God despite the king's decree (6:10–16).

8. **Q:** Who receives the everlasting kingdom in Daniel 7?

   **A:** One like a son of man, coming with the clouds (7:13–14).

9. **Q:** Which angel explained Daniel's visions?

   **A:** Gabriel (8:16; 9:21).

10. **Q:** What does Daniel 12:2 teach?

    **A:** The resurrection of the dead: some to everlasting life, some to everlasting contempt.
