# Nahum

## Summary

Nahum announces the fall of Nineveh, the proud, violent capital of the Assyrian empire that had crushed Israel and terrorised Judah. The Lord is slow to anger but will not leave the guilty unpunished. He is a refuge for those who trust him and an overwhelming flood against his enemies. For oppressed Judah, Nineveh's fall is good news; for Nineveh, there is no healing.

## Setting

- **When:** after Assyria destroyed Thebes in Egypt (663 BC, 3:8) and before Nineveh fell to the Babylonians and Medes in 612 BC. Timmer[^bto] uses these two events to date the book; the *Lutheran Study Bible*[^lsb] gives about 663–612 BC. The *Reformation Study Bible*[^rsb] suggests about 660–650 BC, while Thebes' fall was fresh and Assyria was shaken by revolt, when Judah's King Manasseh was Assyria's loyal vassal; or early in Josiah's reign, before 627 BC.
- **Where:** addressed to Nineveh, but written for the ears of Judah suffering under Assyria. Nahum was from Elkosh, whose location is unknown; suggestions include sites near Nineveh, in Galilee (Capernaum may mean "village of Nahum") and in Judah, which the book itself favours (1:15).
- **Who wrote it:** Nahum the Elkoshite (1:1). His name means "comfort", a short form of Nehemiah, "the Lord comforts" (the *Reformation Heritage KJV Study Bible*, *RH*[^rh]).
- **Fulfilment:** *RH*[^rh] notes that the Babylonian Chronicle describes Nineveh's siege in 612 BC, and that a flooding Tigris helped the attackers breach the walls, much as Nahum pictured (1:8; 2:6).
- **Where it sits:** among the Twelve; in the Greek order it follows Jonah directly. It is a sequel to Jonah: about a century after Nineveh repented, it has returned to violence, and judgment is now certain. Unlike Jonah, there is no sign Nineveh ever heard Nahum's verdict; *RH*[^rh] sees God's freedom in both mercy and judgment (Rom 9:14–18). Like Isaiah 13–23 and Obadiah, it is an oracle against a foreign nation, not mere national revenge: God judges Israel's sins just as surely (*RSB*[^rsb]).

## Structure

The book calls itself both an "oracle" and a "vision" (1:1). Chapter 1 builds to the conflict that chapters 2–3 describe with the rapid, vivid pace of a war report. The *RSB*'s[^rsb] outline:

- 1:1: Heading
- 1:2–14: Nineveh's just and merciful Judge: a hymn to the Lord, jealous and avenging, yet good and a stronghold (1:2–8)
- 1:15–2:13: Nineveh powerless before the Lord's army: good news for Judah, and the city's fall
- 3: Nineveh's irreversible destruction: woe to the bloody city, with taunts and no healing

## Chapter by chapter

- **Nahum 1:** The Lord is a jealous and avenging God, slow to anger but great in power, who will by no means clear the guilty. Mountains quake before him. Yet "the Lord is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble; he knows those who take refuge in him" (1:7). He will end Nineveh's oppression of Judah: "Behold … the feet of him who brings good news, who publishes peace!" (1:15).
- **Nahum 2:** A dramatic picture of Nineveh's fall: attackers in scarlet, chariots racing through the streets, the river gates opened, the palace melting, treasure plundered. The lion's den is empty.
- **Nahum 3:** "Woe to the bloody city, all full of lies and plunder". Nineveh will fall like Thebes did. Its defences crumble like ripe figs, its officials scatter like locusts, and all who hear of its fall clap their hands, for who has not felt its cruelty?

## Key people

- **Nahum the Elkoshite:** the prophet.
- **Nineveh / Assyria:** the oppressive empire and its capital.
- **The king of Assyria:** whose shepherds sleep as his people scatter (3:18).
- **Judah:** the oppressed people who receive good news (1:15).

## Key events

- The fall of Thebes (663 BC), remembered in 3:8–10
- The fall of Nineveh (612 BC), foretold in 2–3

## Themes

- **God's justice against oppressors:** Assyria's cruelty, deceit, greedy trade and lust for conquest will be repaid (3:1–4, 16, 19). God used Assyria to discipline his people, but it went too far (Isa 10:5–12). Timmer[^bto] discusses how the Twelve hold the nations accountable for their sins.
- **God's patience and wrath:** slow to anger, but his patience is not weakness: he won't leave the guilty unpunished (1:3; compare Ex 34:6–7). He rules every event of history.
- **God as refuge:** he is good and a stronghold for those who trust him (1:7).
- **Good news of deliverance:** the end of the oppressor is peace for God's people (1:13, 15). His judgment serves his saving purpose, preserving a remnant until the Messiah comes (*RSB*[^rsb]).
- **God's power over creation and empires:** the strongest empire falls at his word.

## How it points to Christ

Nahum's "feet of him who brings good news, who publishes peace" (1:15) is echoed in Isaiah 52:7, and Paul applies it to those who preach the gospel of Christ (Rom 10:15). Nahum announces the downfall of the oppressor. In Christ, God has "disarmed the rulers and authorities" (Col 2:15) and will finally judge every cruel power, while being a refuge for all who trust him.

The God who is both slow to anger and just shows both perfectly at the cross, where justice and mercy meet (Rom 3:25–26).

## Common mix-ups

- Nahum and Jonah are both about Nineveh, but Jonah records its repentance and Nahum, about a century later, its final judgment.
- Nineveh fell in 612 BC to the Babylonians and Medes, not to Israel.
- "How beautiful … the feet of him who brings good news" appears in both Nahum 1:15 and Isaiah 52:7.
- Nahum's name means "comfort": his message is comfort for Judah, not for Nineveh.

## Going deeper

- **Is Nahum's joy over Nineveh's fall right?** How should we think about rejoicing at the downfall of oppressors? Compare Revelation 18–19.
- **Nahum and Jonah together.** Read Jonah 4:2 beside Nahum 1:3. The same description of God appears in both, applied differently. What does that teach about God's patience and its limits?
- **God as refuge (1:7).** In a book full of judgment, what does this verse offer to those who trust God?
- **The nations in the Twelve.** Timmer[^bto] shows that when the nations resist God and oppress his people, they face punishment. How does Nahum fit, and how does it differ from passages where nations turn to God?

## Q&A

1. **Q:** Which city's fall does Nahum announce?

   **A:** Nineveh, the capital of Assyria (1:1; 2:8).

2. **Q:** When did Nineveh fall?

   **A:** In 612 BC.

3. **Q:** What does the name Nahum mean?

   **A:** "Comfort".

4. **Q:** How is God described in 1:3?

   **A:** Slow to anger and great in power, who will by no means clear the guilty.

5. **Q:** What does Nahum 1:7 say about the Lord?

   **A:** He is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble, and knows those who take refuge in him.

6. **Q:** What good news does 1:15 announce?

   **A:** Peace for Judah: the oppressor is cut off.

7. **Q:** Which city's fall does Nahum use as a warning to Nineveh?

   **A:** Thebes in Egypt (3:8).

8. **Q:** What does Nahum call Nineveh in chapter 3?

   **A:** "The bloody city, all full of lies and plunder" (3:1).

9. **Q:** Which earlier prophet also went to Nineveh?

   **A:** Jonah, a century or more earlier, when Nineveh repented (Jonah 3:3–10).

10. **Q:** How does the book end?

    **A:** With everyone clapping at Nineveh's fall, because all had suffered its cruelty (3:19).
