# Jonah

## Summary

God sends Jonah to warn Nineveh, the capital of Israel's brutal enemy Assyria, but Jonah runs the other way. After a storm and three days inside a great fish, he finally obeys. To his dismay, the whole city repents and God spares it. The book ends with God's question to his angry prophet about compassion for a city of 120,000 people who don't know their right hand from their left (4:11).

## Setting

- **When:** Jonah son of Amittai prophesied in the reign of Jeroboam II of Israel (793–753 BC), foretelling the king's victories that pushed Israel's border back to Solomon's line (2 Kgs 14:25). The Lutheran Study Bible gives about 790 BC. Prosperity hid decay, and Hosea was warning that Assyria would one day conquer Israel, as it did in 722 BC; Jonah may have sensed that saving Nineveh meant trouble for his own nation (Reformation Study Bible).
- **Where:** Israel (Jonah was from Gath-hepher in Galilee), the port of Joppa, the Mediterranean, and Nineveh in Assyria.
- **Who wrote it:** anonymous. The book is told about Jonah in the third person, by Jonah or another writer after the events. Scholars date its writing anywhere from the 8th to the 3rd century BC; the RSB sees no good reason against an early date, before Nineveh fell in 612 BC.
- **Where it sits:** among the Twelve, the only one that is mostly a story about a prophet rather than a collection of his oracles. It is a carefully told story, but the RSB reads it as real history, not allegory or parable: Jewish tradition did, and so did Jesus (Matt 12:39–41). Most objections assume God can't act in nature or history. Timmer notes that in one ancient Qumran copy Jonah may have come last in the collection.

## Structure

Two parallel halves, each opening with God's command to go to Nineveh (Reformation Study Bible):

- 1–2: Jonah disobedient and delivered: flight, storm, sailors, the fish, and Jonah's prayer of thanks
- 3–4: Jonah obedient and rebuked: preaching, Nineveh's repentance, Jonah's anger, and God's lesson

The story builds to its climax in the very last verse, and ends with an unanswered question (4:11). Jonah's grateful prayer for his own rescue (2) sharply contrasts with his anger at Nineveh's rescue (4).

## Chapter by chapter

- **Jonah 1:** God tells Jonah to cry against Nineveh. Jonah boards a ship at Joppa heading for Tarshish, away from the Lord's presence. A great storm threatens the ship, and the pagan sailors pray while Jonah sleeps. Lots fall on Jonah, he tells them to throw him overboard, and the sea calms. The sailors fear the Lord, and a great fish swallows Jonah.
- **Jonah 2:** From inside the fish, Jonah prays a psalm of thanksgiving for rescue from the deep: "Salvation belongs to the Lord!" (2:9). The fish vomits him onto dry land.
- **Jonah 3:** God calls Jonah a second time, and he obeys. "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown". The people believe God, fast and put on sackcloth, from the king down to the animals. God relents from the disaster.
- **Jonah 4:** Jonah is furious: he knew God was "gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love" (4:2), and he wants to die. God appoints a plant to shade him, then a worm to kill it. Jonah pities the plant, and God asks: should I not pity Nineveh? (4:10–11).

## Key people

- **Jonah son of Amittai:** a prophet from Galilee who runs from God's call.
- **The sailors:** pagan mariners who end up fearing and worshipping the Lord (1:16).
- **The king of Nineveh:** who leads his city in repentance (3:6–9).
- **The Ninevites:** the people of Assyria's capital, notorious for cruelty, who repent.

## Key events

- Jonah's flight to Tarshish (1)
- The storm, and Jonah thrown overboard (1)
- Three days and nights in the great fish (1:17)
- Jonah's prayer (2)
- Nineveh repents and God relents (3)
- The plant, the worm, and God's question (4)

## Themes

- **God's mercy reaches the nations:** God cares even for Israel's enemies. Timmer discusses how the Twelve show some among the nations turning to God and being delivered.
- **The prophet's prejudice:** Jonah's problem isn't doubt about God's power but resentment of his grace to outsiders (4:2). He wants mercy for himself and justice for his enemies; God's justice is impartial and his forgiveness can reach anyone (RSB).
- **Repentance:** Jews read Jonah on the Day of Atonement. Nineveh's repentance (3:5–10) is held up by Jesus as a rebuke to his own hearers. It also shows that God's warnings of judgment can be conditional (3:10; Jer 18:7–8). Timmer notes that "who knows?" (3:9) shows repentance can't presume on God's mercy.
- **God's sovereignty over creation:** God appoints a storm, a fish, a plant, a worm and a wind.
- **Salvation belongs to the Lord:** deliverance is God's gift, to Jonah and to Nineveh alike (2:9).

## How it points to Christ

Jesus called his resurrection "the sign of Jonah": as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the fish, so the Son of Man would be three days in the heart of the earth (Matt 12:39–41). He added that the men of Nineveh will rise up in judgment against his generation, because they repented at Jonah's preaching, "and behold, something greater than Jonah is here".

Unlike Jonah, who fled from God's mission to the nations, Jesus willingly went to death, and sent his disciples to preach repentance to all nations (Luke 24:46–47). Jonah sleeps in the storm in disobedience; Jesus sleeps in the storm in perfect trust, then calms it (Mark 4:35–41).

## Common mix-ups

- The Bible says "a great fish", not specifically a whale.
- Jonah went toward Tarshish (probably in Spain), the opposite direction from Nineveh.
- Nineveh repented under Jonah, but about 150 years later Nahum announced its destruction (612 BC).
- Jonah's prayer in chapter 2 is a thanksgiving for being rescued from drowning, prayed inside the fish.
- Jonah is angry at the end, not because his prophecy failed, but because God was merciful to Assyria.
- Jonah came from Galilee (Gath-hepher), despite the Pharisees' claim that no prophet comes from there (John 7:52).

## Going deeper

- **Why did Jonah run?** Read 4:2. What does Jonah's anger reveal about him, and about us when God shows mercy to people we dislike?
- **The pagan sailors and Ninevites.** Both outsiders respond to God better than the prophet. How does the book contrast them with Jonah?
- **"Who knows? God may turn" (3:9).** How does Nineveh's repentance compare with Joel 2:14? What does it teach about repentance without presumption?
- **History or parable?** Some read Jonah as a parable. Jesus treats Jonah and Nineveh as real (Matt 12:39–41). What is at stake?
- **The unanswered question (4:11).** The book ends with God's question and no reply from Jonah. Why? How should the reader answer?

## Q&A

1. **Q:** Where did God send Jonah?

   **A:** To Nineveh, the capital of Assyria (1:2).

2. **Q:** Where did Jonah try to go instead?

   **A:** Tarshish, by ship from Joppa (1:3).

3. **Q:** What did the sailors do to find out who caused the storm?

   **A:** They cast lots, and the lot fell on Jonah (1:7).

4. **Q:** How long was Jonah inside the great fish?

   **A:** Three days and three nights (1:17).

5. **Q:** What did Jonah declare at the end of his prayer?

   **A:** "Salvation belongs to the Lord!" (2:9).

6. **Q:** What message did Jonah preach in Nineveh?

   **A:** "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown" (3:4).

7. **Q:** How did Nineveh respond?

   **A:** The people believed God, fasted and put on sackcloth, from the king down (3:5–9).

8. **Q:** Why was Jonah angry?

   **A:** Because God was gracious and spared Nineveh (4:1–2).

9. **Q:** What did God use to teach Jonah a lesson?

   **A:** A plant, a worm and a scorching east wind (4:6–8).

10. **Q:** What did Jesus call "the sign of Jonah"?

    **A:** His three days in the grave before his resurrection (Matt 12:39–40).
