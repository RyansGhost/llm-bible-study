# Psalms

## Summary

Psalms is Israel's book of prayer and praise: 150 songs and prayers written over about a thousand years. Its laments, thanksgivings, hymns, royal songs and wisdom poems teach God's people to bring every experience to God and to live the blessed life he intends (Futato). Its great theme is that the Lord reigns as King, and that his anointed King, the Son of David, is coming.

## Setting

- **When:** from Moses (Psalm 90, perhaps about 1400 BC) through David and the monarchy to after the exile (Psalm 137 looks back on Babylon). The collection reached its final form after the exile (Futato). The Reformation Heritage KJV Study Bible (RH) puts the span at roughly 1445–444 BC, reading Psalm 147's rebuilt Jerusalem as Nehemiah's day (147:2, 13).
- **Where:** in Israel's life of worship, at the tabernacle and temple, in battle, in exile, and in private distress.
- **Who wrote it:** many authors. David is linked with about 73 psalms; others are "of" Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses, Heman, Ethan, and many are anonymous. Futato notes that "of David" could grammatically mean "for" or "about" David. The Reformation Study Bible holds that the titles are original and reliable and do name the author: Psalm 18's title matches 2 Samuel 22:1, and Scripture remembers David as Israel's singer (2 Sam 23:1; Matt 22:43). Fourteen titles give the event behind the psalm (such as 3, 18, 51). RH also counts the titles as part of inspired Scripture, since Jesus and the apostles argue from them (Matt 22:43–44; Acts 2:29–31).
- **Where it sits:** first of the Writings, the third part of the Hebrew Bible. Jesus refers to "the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms" (Luke 24:44). It is the Old Testament book the New Testament quotes most.

## Structure

- **Five books,** each closing with a doxology (41:13; 72:18–19; 89:52; 106:48), with Psalms 146–150 as the grand finale:
    - Book 1: Psalms 1–41
    - Book 2: Psalms 42–72
    - Book 3: Psalms 73–89
    - Book 4: Psalms 90–106
    - Book 5: Psalms 107–150
- **A two-part introduction:** Psalm 1 (the blessed life of delighting in God's instruction) and Psalm 2 (the Lord and his Anointed King) (Futato).
- **Five books, like the five books of Moses:** both are God's instruction (*torah*) for his people (1:2) (RSB).
- **From lament to praise:** laments cluster early and praise grows toward the end, which is why the Hebrew title is "Book of Praises" even though laments are the most common type (Futato).
- **The story of the five books:** following scholarship since 1985, Futato reads the books as a storyline. Book 1 opens with God's covenant with his King (2); Book 2 ends with the kingship passing to Solomon (72); Book 3 ends darkly, with Psalm 88 (the only psalm with no praise) and Psalm 89's lament that David's crown lies in the dust. Books 4 and 5 answer the exiles' question, how to live without a son of David on the throne: by trusting that "the Lord reigns" (93; 95–99), and by obedience (119) and true worship (145–150), with David as a model (138–145) and the Messiah promised (110) (RSB).

## Chapter by chapter

With 150 psalms, this section goes book by book and highlights the psalms most worth knowing.

- **Book 1 (Psalms 1–41):** mostly David's psalms, many of them laments.
    - 1: two ways, the righteous and the wicked
    - 2: the Lord's Anointed, his Son
    - 8: human dignity under God's majesty
    - 16: "you will not abandon my soul to Sheol"
    - 19: God speaks in the heavens and in his law
    - 22: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"
    - 23: "The Lord is my shepherd"
    - 24: the King of glory
    - 27: "The Lord is my light"
    - 32: the joy of forgiveness
    - 34: "Taste and see"
    - 37: don't fret over evildoers
    - 40: "I delight to do your will"
- **Book 2 (Psalms 42–72):** psalms of the sons of Korah and of David, ending "the prayers of David … are ended" (72:20).
    - 42–43: longing for God like a thirsty deer
    - 45: a royal wedding song
    - 46: "God is our refuge and strength", the basis of Luther's "A Mighty Fortress"
    - 51: David's prayer of repentance

    - 67: may the nations praise you
    - 69: zeal for God's house
    - 72: the righteous king's worldwide reign

    - 73: the prosperity of the wicked, until the psalmist enters God's sanctuary
    - 78: lessons from Israel's history
    - 84: "How lovely is your dwelling place"
    - 88: the darkest lament, ending in darkness
    - 89: God's covenant with David seems broken
- **Book 4 (Psalms 90–106):** an answer to the crisis.
    - 90: Moses on God's eternity and our frailty
    - 91: shelter under the Most High
    - 93 and 95–99: "the Lord reigns"
    - 100: "make a joyful noise"
    - 103: "Bless the Lord, O my soul"
    - 104: creation praise

- **Book 5 (Psalms 107–150):** hope and praise.
    - 107: the redeemed give thanks
    - 110: the priest-king at God's right hand
    - 113–118: the "Hallel", sung at Passover
    - 117: the shortest psalm
    - 118: the rejected stone becomes the cornerstone
    - 119: the longest psalm, an acrostic in praise of God's word
    - 120–134: the Songs of Ascents, for pilgrims going up to Jerusalem: 121, 127, 130, 133
    - 137: by the rivers of Babylon
    - 139: God knows and searches me
    - 145: David's last psalm of praise
    - 146–150: "Praise the Lord!", the closing doxology

## Key people

- **David:** the psalmist most closely linked with the book, and the king whose covenant runs through it. Some titles tie psalms to events in his life (Pss 3; 18; 51).
- **Asaph:** a Levite musician under David; Psalms 50 and 73–83.
- **The sons of Korah:** Levite singers; Psalms 42–49, 84–85 and 87–88.
- **Moses:** Psalm 90.
- **Solomon:** Psalms 72 and 127.
- **Heman and Ethan:** wise men and musicians; Psalms 88 and 89.
- **The Lord's Anointed:** the king of Psalm 2, 45, 72, 89 and 110, fulfilled in Christ.

## Key events

The Psalms aren't a narrative, but they recall many events:

- Creation (8; 104)
- The exodus and wilderness (78; 105; 106; 114)
- God's covenant with David (89; 132)
- David's sin with Bathsheba (51)
- David fleeing from Absalom (3)
- Pilgrimages to Jerusalem (120–134)
- The fall of Jerusalem and the exile (74; 79; 137)

## Themes

- **The whole soul in worship:** RH's summary of the theme: the Psalms engage every part of a person in worshipping God, and were given to guide his people's praises and prayers, together and alone.
- **The Lord reigns:** Futato calls God's kingship the dominant theme: "The Lord reigns" (93:1), and his King is coming (2; 110). God is King, Creator and Redeemer. Luther called the Psalms a "little Bible", a summary of the Old Testament.
- **The King's hymnbook:** Israel's king led its worship, so many psalms belong first to David's line and supremely to Christ, who prays them (22) and to whom they are sung (Heb 2:12) (RSB).
- **The blessed life:** "Blessed is the man" (1:1). The book teaches the way of life and well-being by delighting in God's instruction. Futato notes that Psalm 1 begins with "blessed" and ends with "perish".
- **Kinds of psalm:** hymns of praise (103), laments (6), thanksgivings for answered prayer (116), songs of confidence (23), royal psalms about God's or David's kingship (2; 47), and wisdom psalms (1; 37) (RSB).
- **Honest lament:** the most common type of psalm. God's people may bring fear, grief, anger and doubt to him (13; 22; 88). Nearly every lament turns to trust by the end.
- **Cries for justice:** some psalms ask God to punish the wicked (69; 109). Christians are told to bless personal enemies (Rom 12:14–21); these prayers are for flagrant, unrepentant enemies of God's people, and must leave room for God to save them as he saved us (RSB).
- **Praise and thanksgiving:** the book moves from lament to praise, ending in a crescendo of hallelujahs (146–150).
- **God's word:** Psalms 1, 19 and 119 delight in God's law as life-giving instruction.
- **Repentance and forgiveness:** Psalms 32, 51 and 130; since early times seven have been grouped as the "penitential psalms" (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143).
- **The Passover Hallel:** Psalms 113–118 were sung at Passover, and were probably the hymn Jesus and the disciples sang after the Last Supper (Matt 26:30) (RH).
- **Trust in God as refuge:** Psalms 23, 46, 62 and 91.
- **The nations:** the Psalms call all peoples to praise Israel's God (67; 96; 117).

Calvin called the Psalms "an anatomy of all the parts of the soul", because every human emotion appears in them (Commentary on Psalms, preface).

## How it points to Christ

Jesus said everything written about him in "the Psalms" must be fulfilled (Luke 24:44). He is the Son and Anointed King of Psalm 2 (Acts 13:33; Heb 1:5), the Son of Man of Psalm 8 (Heb 2:6–9), and the Holy One not abandoned to the grave in Psalm 16 (Acts 2:25–31). He prayed Psalm 22 on the cross (Matt 27:46), and the details of that psalm match his suffering. RH counts four of his seven sayings from the cross as quoting or fulfilling the Psalms (22:1; 69:21; 22:31; 31:5). Where a messianic psalm confesses sin (40:12; compare 40:6–8 and Heb 10:5–7), RH explains that the sinless Christ speaks as the one on whom his people's sins were laid (Isa 53:6; 2 Cor 5:21). He is the royal bridegroom of Psalm 45 (Heb 1:8–9), the priest-king seated at God's right hand in Psalm 110, the psalm the New Testament quotes most (Matt 22:44; Heb 5–7), and the rejected stone made cornerstone of Psalm 118 (Matt 21:42).

Christians have long prayed the Psalms with Christ: he is both the one the Psalms speak of and the true worshipper who prays them perfectly.

## Common mix-ups

- David didn't write every psalm. About half are linked to him; others are by Asaph, the sons of Korah, Moses, Solomon and others, or are anonymous.
- Psalm numbering differs in older Catholic and Orthodox Bibles (following the Greek and Latin), which are often one number behind (for example, their Psalm 22 is our 23).
- Psalm 119 is the longest chapter in the Bible; Psalm 117 is the shortest.
- "Selah" is probably a musical or liturgical marker. Its exact meaning is uncertain.
- Psalm 22 (forsaken) and Psalm 23 (the shepherd) sit side by side but are very different.
- The "Songs of Ascents" (120–134) are pilgrim songs, not about climbing stairs inside the temple.
- The Psalms were sung and prayed, not just read. Many have musical directions in their titles.

## Going deeper

- **Why is it called the "Book of Praises" when laments dominate?** Futato shows how the book moves from lament to praise. What does that teach about the life of faith?
- **The five books as a story.** How does Psalm 89's cry over David's fallen throne lead into Book 4's "the Lord reigns"? Where does Book 5 put its hope? Read: Futato, Five Books and Our King Is Coming.
- **Psalms 1 and 2 as the doorway.** Futato reads them as a two-part introduction: instruction for the blessed life, and the reign of God's King. How do they frame the rest?
- **The imprecatory psalms.** Psalms 35, 69, 109 and 137 call down judgment on enemies. How should Christians understand and pray them in light of Jesus' command to love enemies? Note that they hand vengeance to God.
- **Praying the Psalms.** Calvin and Luther urged Christians to use the Psalms as their prayer book. Try praying a lament (13), a confession (51) and a praise psalm (103) this week.
- **Christ in the Psalms.** Trace Psalms 2, 22, 110 and 118 through the Gospels and Hebrews.

## Q&A

1. **Q:** How many psalms are there, and how are they divided?

   **A:** 150, in five books (1–41, 42–72, 73–89, 90–106, 107–150).

2. **Q:** What does the Hebrew title of Psalms mean?

   **A:** "Praises", the Book of Praises.

3. **Q:** Which psalm may be the oldest, and who wrote it?

   **A:** Psalm 90, "a prayer of Moses, the man of God" (its title).

4. **Q:** What is the most common type of psalm?

   **A:** The lament, such as Psalms 3, 13 and 22.

5. **Q:** Which psalm did Jesus quote on the cross?

   **A:** Psalm 22: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (22:1; Matt 27:46).

6. **Q:** Which psalm did David write after his sin with Bathsheba?

   **A:** Psalm 51 (its title; 2 Sam 12:1–13).

7. **Q:** What is the longest psalm, and what is it about?

   **A:** Psalm 119, celebrating God's word.

8. **Q:** Which psalms are the Songs of Ascents?

   **A:** Psalms 120–134, sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem.

9. **Q:** Which psalm is quoted most in the New Testament?

   **A:** Psalm 110: "The Lord says to my Lord: sit at my right hand" (110:1; Matt 22:44; Heb 1:13).

10. **Q:** How does the book of Psalms end?

    **A:** With Psalms 146–150, each beginning and ending "Praise the Lord!"
